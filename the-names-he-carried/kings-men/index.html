The King’s Men: Laurence’s Ancestors and the Kings of Scotland

Research notes, not part of the book · what the records show, and what they don’t

Laurence Brown Douglas was born in 1907 to a domestic servant and registered under her surname, Brown. But the family tree traced today shows that for more than five hundred years, his ancestors stood close to the kings of Scotland. One kept the king’s chamber in the 1100s. Another died on the day a king fell at Flodden. Another wrote the law for Charles I, then helped draw up the National Covenant that defied him. And his Gordon forefathers were shot, hunted, sentenced to death and imprisoned on the Bass Rock for refusing the Crown’s rule over their church.

First, the honest answer: is Laurence descended from a king?

Not that we have found. No line traced so far reaches a king or queen. The connection is service, marriage and conflict. These were the lairds, judges and officers around the throne, and the men who stood against it. Old Scottish landed families often do lead back to royalty somewhere, and some of these lines haven’t been followed to the end. If a royal descent turns up, it will be reported here, with its sources.

King David I and the royal chamberlain (1100s)

The oldest name in Laurence’s family tree belongs to the court of David I, King of Scots from 1124 to 1153, the king who founded abbeys and burghs across Scotland. The published pedigree of the Chalmers of Gadgirth begins with Herbert de Camera, “of the chamber,” chamberlain to King David. The chamberlain ran the king’s household and collected his revenues, one of the highest offices in the kingdom. The family took its name, Chalmers, from that office. A Reginald de Camera held Gadgirth in Ayrshire under King William the Lion (1165–1214).

How sure is this? It is the family’s published tradition, printed in Burke’s Landed Gentry and James Paterson’s History of the County of Ayr. The sources disagree about several generations in the middle, so the link to the chamberlain is a tradition, not a proven descent. The later Chalmers generations, down to Mary Chalmers, who married John Gordon of Earlstoun in 1585, are much better recorded.

Fighting for the French king (1429)

The same pedigree records a John Chalmers of Gadgirth killed at the Battle of the Herrings in 1429. That battle was fought in France during the Hundred Years’ War, when Scottish soldiers served the French king against the English.

A princess in the family (1400s)

About 1440, Sir John Chalmers of Gadgirth married Elizabeth Hamilton, a daughter of Sir James Hamilton of Cadzow. Her brother became the first Lord Hamilton and married Princess Mary Stewart, a daughter of King James II. The Hamiltons grew into one of the most powerful families in Scotland. This makes the royal family in-laws of Laurence’s ancestors. It is not a royal descent: Laurence descends from Elizabeth, not from the princess.

Flodden: the day a king died (1513)

On September 9, 1513, King James IV led the Scottish army against the English at Flodden in Northumberland. The king and much of Scotland’s nobility were killed. On that same day John Murray, Laurence’s thirteenth great-grandfather and the father of Sir Andrew Murray of Blackbarony, died. Many Scottish lairds fell at Flodden, but we have not yet confirmed that John was one of them. The date is recorded; the place of his death is not.

A queen’s arrival and a lawyer for the king (1537–1646)

Family tradition says the Hopes came to Scotland from France in 1537 in the train of Madeleine de Valois, the young French bride of King James V. Modern researchers doubt the story and point to an Edinburgh merchant named John Hope instead. Either way, the family’s great man was Sir Thomas Hope of Craighall (1573–1646), Laurence’s tenth great-grandfather. He was Lord Advocate to King Charles I, the Crown’s chief law officer in Scotland, and was made a baronet in 1628.

Then came the twist. When Charles I tried to force a new prayer book on the Church of Scotland, the country rose against him. In 1638 the National Covenant was drawn up, and Thomas Hope, the king’s own Lord Advocate, is credited with making sure its wording was not treason. His son Sir John Hope, Lord Craighall, became a judge of the Court of Session.

The Covenanters who defied the Crown (1638–1689)

The Covenant divided Scotland for fifty years, and the Gordons of Earlstoun were on the side that suffered. Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun (1587–1654) sat as an elder in the Glasgow General Assembly of 1638, which defied Charles I. His son William Gordon (1614–1679) refused the bishops imposed under Charles II, was fined and lost his estate, and in June 1679 was shot dead by English dragoons while riding to join the Covenanter army at Bothwell Bridge.

William’s son, Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun (1650–1726), fought at Bothwell Bridge and escaped. He fled to Holland and was condemned to death in his absence. Captured at sea in 1683, he was sentenced to be beheaded but spared, and spent six years in Edinburgh Castle, on the Bass Rock and in Blackness Castle. In 1689 the Revolution drove King James VII from his throne and set him free. His wife’s brother, Sir Robert Hamilton of Preston, was one of the Covenanter leaders at Bothwell Bridge. Sir Alexander’s daughter Mary was Laurence’s sixth great-grandmother.

The family’s defiance went back further still. Alexander Gordon of Airds (1479–1580), Laurence’s twelfth great-grandfather, read Wycliffe’s English New Testament aloud to his tenants in the wood of Airds. In the early 1500s, when the Church and the Crown treated that as heresy, it was a dangerous thing to do.

The kings in Laurence’s family story

  • David I (1124–1153): served by Herbert de Camera, his chamberlain (family tradition).
  • William the Lion (1165–1214): Reginald de Camera held Gadgirth under him (family tradition).
  • James II (1437–1460): his daughter married the brother of Elizabeth Hamilton, Laurence’s ancestor.
  • James IV (1488–1513): killed at Flodden on the day John Murray died.
  • James V (1513–1542): the Hope family’s tradition ties its arrival to his French queen.
  • Charles I (1625–1649): served by Sir Thomas Hope as Lord Advocate, and defied by the Covenant he helped frame.
  • Charles II and James VII (1660–1688): under them the Gordons of Earlstoun were fined, shot, condemned and imprisoned.
  • William and Mary (1689): their Revolution freed Sir Alexander Gordon from prison.

What we know, and what we don’t

  • Well documented: Sir Thomas Hope as Lord Advocate; the Gordons of Earlstoun as Covenanters; Sir Alexander Gordon’s imprisonment and release; Mary Gordon as his daughter (her gravestone says so).
  • Published tradition: the Chalmers descent from Herbert de Camera; the Hopes’ arrival with Queen Madeleine.
  • Not yet confirmed: whether John Murray died at Flodden.
  • The key link still to prove: that Alexander Goldie (born 1762) was the son of James Goldie of Craigmuie. Every story on this page reaches Laurence through that one baptism record. See the Bloodline Report.
