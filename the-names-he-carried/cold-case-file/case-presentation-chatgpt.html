Case presentation: who fathered Laurence?

Written by ChatGPT (OpenAI), November 22, 2026, in answer to Doug Garceau's question: "If you were a police detective or prosecutor, how would you present this case to a grand jury?" Reproduced as given, with headings formatted and one distance shown in miles. Source labels at the ends of paragraphs refer to exhibits in this file. This is an analysis, not a finding of fact, and accuses no one of a crime.

I’d present Binnie Dunlop as a specific man worth investigating. Your mother’s account gives the father’s occupation. The records independently identify a doctor in the family that owned the estate where Agnes gave her home address. That gives the investigation a name and a place. The unresolved question is whether Binnie and Agnes ever met.

What I’d say to the grand jury

“Members of the grand jury, on June 14, 1907, at nine in the evening, Agnes Brown gave birth to a boy at the Royal Maternity Hospital in Edinburgh. She registered him as Lawrence Brown. She signed as his mother, gave her occupation as domestic servant, and gave her domicile as Gean Cottage, Shieldhill, Biggar. The space for his father contains no name. Shieldhill enters this case through Agnes’s own statement. Original birth entry, no. 839

“Laurence’s daughter says she knows her father’s biological father was a doctor. I would put her account before you in her own words. The first question I would ask her is, ‘How did you first learn that he was a doctor, and what exactly do you remember being said?’ If she heard it from Laurence himself, that tells us something different from hearing it from another relative. Her account, as we have it today, identifies an occupation. Binnie’s name comes from the investigation.

“Why Binnie? The 1861 census index lists Nathaniel and James Dunlop as sons in the same household. James’s 1901 medical obituary identifies Dr. Binnie Dunlop as his son. Binnie was therefore a physician and the Shieldhill owner’s nephew. His 1907 Medical Register entry gives 16 Carlton Place, Glasgow. His former school’s register, published in 1905, also gives Charteris House, Woodford Green, Essex. Those records establish his qualifications and addresses. Neither puts him at Shieldhill in the autumn of 1906. A guest room made a visit possible. A dated letter from Shieldhill, a guest-book entry, or an account naming him would establish that a visit actually happened. 1861 household · James’s obituary · 1907 medical register · 1905 school register

“The Archibald connection goes back to Agnes’s childhood. In 1891, five-year-old Agnes appears in household schedule 7. Mary and Alexander Archibald appear in schedule 5, with one household enumerated between them. In 1901, Mary was a sewing mistress at Gean Tree Cottages, and Agnes lived with her uncle at Lime Tree Cottage. That same year Alexander was recorded at Shieldhill as a domestic gardener, heading a household with his wife Annie and a servant, Susan Cumisky. These entries give us a sound reason to investigate whether Mary helped Agnes. They leave open who occupied Agnes’s particular cottage in 1907, who attended her pregnancy, and where on the estate Alexander slept. 1891 households · 1901 cottage households · Alexander’s household

“Then there is Edinburgh. On April 2, 1911, three-year-old Laurence B. Douglas was listed as ‘Son’ in the Douglas household at 16 Lochrin Place. Your point about the distance holds up. The former maternity hospital at 79 Lauriston Place stands about one-fifth of a mile straight-line from the centre of the modern postcode covering No. 16. That is a postcode estimate rather than a door-to-door measurement. The uncertainty that matters more is when. Nearly four years separate the birth and the census. We know where Laurence was born and where he was living by age three. We still need the first dated record that puts him in Lizzie’s care.” 1911 census page portal.historicenvironment.scot

The question I’d press hardest

Did Agnes go to Edinburgh because someone had already arranged for Lizzie to take the baby? That is a sharper question than why Agnes passed up Glasgow. Edinburgh’s Royal Maternity Hospital regularly admitted unmarried women. On the very page where Agnes registered Laurence, the next entry records another unmarried domestic servant who delivered at the same hospital six days later. Agnes had a reason to seek care there independent of either family. What would distinguish her case is a record placing Lizzie, Mary, a Dunlop, or another named intermediary in the arrangements for Agnes or the baby. Birth-register page, entries 839–840 pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

I’d pursue two questions separately. For paternity, I’d preserve your mother’s account before introducing Binnie’s name, look for a contemporary record of contact between Binnie and Agnes, and check for any claim Agnes made for the child’s support. For placement, I’d start with Agnes’s hospital casebook and birth-register entry, then establish the Douglases’ address in June 1907. The hospital’s registers of this period generally omitted the father’s details for an unmarried patient, so a blank there would carry little weight. A referral, a second address, or a named contact could be far more revealing. PMC

My finding would be that Binnie fits the family testimony and has a documented family connection to the place Agnes named. That warrants pursuing him by name. The Edinburgh evidence gives you a second, concrete lead into how Laurence came to Lizzie. A record connecting those two events could change the case. Until then, who fathered Laurence and who arranged his care remain two questions with potentially different answers.
