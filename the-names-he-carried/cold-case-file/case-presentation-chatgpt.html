Case presentation: who fathered Laurence?

Sir Nathaniel Dunlop has the stronger documented connection to the estate gatherings of 1906. His nephew Binnie was a physician, which makes him relevant to the family account. The July newspaper places Nathaniel at Shieldhill. No source presented here places either man with Agnes. The Case for Sir Nathaniel Dunlop brings together the records.

What the records establish

On June 14, 1907, at nine in the evening, Agnes Brown gave birth in Edinburgh. She signed the registration as Lawrence Brown’s mother, recorded her occupation as domestic servant and gave Gean Cottage, Shieldhill as her usual home. The father’s entry is blank.

Laurence’s daughter described “a doctor at the estate”. She did not identify a Dunlop by name. Nathaniel acquired an honorary doctorate after the estimated conception. Binnie already held a medical qualification. Who first used the description, and when, matters to its interpretation.

The 1861 household and Dr James Dunlop’s 1901 obituary establish the kinship. Binnie’s 1907 medical register gives a Glasgow address. His former school’s register in 1905 gives an Essex address. Neither is a record of attendance at Shieldhill.

“The Archibald connection goes back to Agnes’s childhood. In 1891, five-year-old Agnes appears in household schedule 7. Mary and Alexander Archibald appear in schedule 5, with one household enumerated between them. In 1901, Mary was a sewing mistress at Gean Tree Cottages, and Agnes lived with her uncle at Lime Tree Cottage. That same year Alexander was recorded at Shieldhill as a domestic gardener, heading a household with his wife Annie and a servant, Susan Cumisky. These entries give us a sound reason to investigate whether Mary helped Agnes. They leave open who occupied Agnes’s particular cottage in 1907, who attended her pregnancy, and where on the estate Alexander slept. 1891 households · 1901 cottage households · Alexander’s household

“Then there is Edinburgh. On April 2, 1911, three-year-old Laurence B. Douglas was listed as ‘Son’ in the Douglas household at 16 Lochrin Place. Your point about the distance holds up. The former maternity hospital at 79 Lauriston Place stands about one-fifth of a mile straight-line from the centre of the modern postcode covering No. 16. That is a postcode estimate rather than a door-to-door measurement. The uncertainty that matters more is when. Nearly four years separate the birth and the census. We know where Laurence was born and where he was living by age three. We still need the first dated record that puts him in Lizzie’s care.” 1911 census page portal.historicenvironment.scot

The question I’d press hardest

Did Agnes go to Edinburgh because someone had already arranged for Lizzie to take the baby? That is a sharper question than why Agnes passed up Glasgow. Edinburgh’s Royal Maternity Hospital regularly admitted unmarried women. On the very page where Agnes registered Laurence, the next entry records another unmarried domestic servant who delivered at the same hospital six days later. Agnes had a reason to seek care there independent of either family. What would distinguish her case is a record placing Lizzie, Mary, a Dunlop, or another named intermediary in the arrangements for Agnes or the baby. Birth-register page, entries 839–840 pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

For paternity, the priorities are a contemporary acknowledgment or support record and dated evidence of contact with Agnes. For placement, the hospital register and evidence about the Douglas household may explain how Laurence reached Lizzie. These questions could have different answers.

The evidence places Sir Nathaniel at a 1906 estate gathering and identifies Binnie as his physician nephew. It warrants pursuing Sir Nathaniel first. The Edinburgh evidence can help trace the child’s care. It has yet to identify his father.

New evidence: Shieldhill Hall, community activities, and possible intermediaries

Source supplied for verification: Hamilton Advertiser, 6 April 1907, “An Interesting Amateur Entertainment.” The author has supplied excerpts and a summary; the complete newspaper page and its original image must be attached before this is classified as a verified full transcription.

The newspaper account describes winter evening classes at the Quothquan hall, reportedly begun more than a year before publication, approximately early 1906. A governess from Glasgow taught twice weekly. Activities included music, singing and needlework for young women, with a daytime social hour for mothers and older women. The account mentions farm servants learning musical notation, a choir associated with Sunday evening services, and Ellen Dunlop playing the organ.

At a concert reported as taking place the preceding Friday, approximately 29 March 1907, the audience reportedly numbered about 200. Nathaniel Dunlop chaired the gathering and Ellen Dunlop played the piano. This establishes a reported instance of the estate owner personally participating in the hall's activities during Agnes Brown's pregnancy. It does not place him at Shieldhill at the estimated conception date in autumn 1906, nor identify Agnes or Dr Binnie Dunlop among attendees.

What the evidence does and does not establish

  • Documented in the reported article: repeated instruction and community gatherings; women and servants participated as groups; Nathaniel and Ellen were personally involved in the March 1907 concert.
  • Opportunity, not attendance: Agnes was a young domestic servant who gave Gean Cottage as her home in July 1907. Her proximity and demographic fit make participation possible, but the article does not name her, Mary Archibald, Alexander Archibald, the Douglases, or Binnie Dunlop.
  • Not a documented singles event: the article describes education, music, choir and social activities, not organized matchmaking.
  • Construction hypothesis: Nathaniel and Ellen financed the hall in 1903. Nathaniel may have visited during construction, but no dated building-site attendance, contractor account, or supervision record has been identified. Financing does not prove how often he visited.
  • Placement hypothesis: a potential chain through Ellen, the Archibald family, church or hospital contacts is worth testing. No record presently shows who financed Agnes's cottage, travel, care or Laurence's placement.
  • 1929 travel: Laurence's passage occurred 22 years later, after Nathaniel's death. Existing ship and border documentation reportedly identifies self-payment; the original fare fields should be retained as exhibits rather than presenting an estate-funded passage as fact.
