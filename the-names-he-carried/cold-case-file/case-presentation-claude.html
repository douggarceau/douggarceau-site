Case presentation: who fathered Laurence?

Written by Claude (Anthropic), November 22, 2026, at Doug Garceau's request. Question put: "If you were a police detective or prosecutor, how would you present this case to a grand jury?" This is an analysis of the exhibits in this file. It is not a finding of fact and accuses no one of a crime.

Opening statement

No one is charged with a crime. A child born outside marriage in 1907 was not a crime, and everyone in this story has died. The narrow question is whether the evidence justifies a full investigation into whether Dr Binnie Dunlop was Laurence's father, and the records that would settle it. Each link below is ordinary on its own. Together they form a pattern that is hard to dismiss. The gaps are stated too.

Part 1. The family that owned the place where Agnes lived

  • Sir Nathaniel Dunlop owned Shieldhill, a large country house in Lanarkshire, from 1898. He was knighted in July 1907, one month after Laurence was born.
  • 1861 census, 50 Abbotsford Place, Glasgow: Archibald Dunlop's household includes sons Nathaniel, 31, and James, 27, physician and surgeon. James was the knight's brother.
  • Binnie Dunlop was born 3 August 1874 to Dr James Dunlop and Jane Menzies Binnie. James's obituary (Glasgow Medical Journal, April 1901) names "Dr. Binnie Dunlop" as his son. Binnie was the knight's nephew. That relationship is proven.
  • Medical Registers: Binnie qualified in 1898. From 1899 through 1907 his registered address was 16 Carlton Place, Glasgow. From 1908 it was Woodford Green, Essex, England. The baby was born in June 1907; within the next register year the doctor was over 300 miles away. People move for many reasons, but the timing is worth noting.

Part 2. The gardener and his sister

  • 1901 census: Alexander Archibald, 30, domestic gardener at Shieldhill, living there with his wife Annie and a servant. He worked for the Dunlops.
  • Where the gardener lived (added after review): the 1901 census lists Alexander's household at the address "Shieldhill" itself (Libberton ED 3, schedule 3), not at a lodge or cottage, and the household kept its own live-in servant, Susan Cumisky, 26, which is unusual for a gardener. On census night Sir Nathaniel was in Glasgow, at 1 Montgomerie Crescent, Partick, with his daughter Ellen and seven servants. So in 1901 the gardener's family was the household recorded at Shieldhill while the owner was away, which suggests they may have been living in, or keeping, the house. The census does not say which building they occupied, and no record yet shows the gardener and the knight under the same roof. If they were in the house, the gardener's family would have been the people who knew who came and went at Shieldhill.
  • Mary Archibald, born 23 October 1864, Alexander's sister, was at Gean Tree Cottages in 1901.
  • Agnes Brown as a neighbor: in 1891 Agnes, age 5, was listed one household from Alexander and Mary. In 1901 she was at Lime Tree Cottage, close to Mary. The Archibalds had known her since she was a small child.
  • Laurence's 1907 birth entry: Agnes, domestic servant, gives her home as Gean Cottage, Shieldhill, and signs as the mother. No father is named.

Part 3. The choice of hospital

Most women gave birth at home. National Records of Scotland estimates that about 95% of all births in this period, among rich and poor alike, were home births (National Records of Scotland, "Safe delivery: a history of Scottish midwives"). Agnes had a home: on the birth entry she gave her domicile as Gean Cottage, Shieldhill, the estate owned by the knight, Binnie Dunlop's uncle. She could have given birth there with a local midwife, as most women did. Instead she went about 25 miles to a hospital in another city. That makes the hospital birth itself unusual, before even asking why it was Edinburgh.

Agnes's three choices. (1) Give birth at home at Gean Cottage, as about 95% of women did. (2) Go to Glasgow, the nearest city, the doctor's city, with a maternity hospital known for taking unmarried mothers. (3) Go to Edinburgh, a city where, as far as the records show, neither she nor the Dunlops had any tie. She passed over the two obvious choices and took the third. Which Glasgow hospitals Binnie worked at, if any, is not yet in the record; his registered address in Glasgow, 16 Carlton Place, is.

Laurence was born 14 June 1907 at the Royal Maternity Hospital, 79 Lauriston Place, Edinburgh. From the Shieldhill area, Glasgow was the obvious hospital. The Glasgow Royal Maternity Hospital (Rottenrow) was known for caring for unmarried mothers. Glasgow and Edinburgh were each roughly 25–30 miles from Shieldhill, so distance does not explain the choice. Cost does not either: the Edinburgh hospital's care appears to have been free before Maternity Benefit. Glasgow was where Binnie lived and practised and where the Dunlop name was known in medicine. The argument is that Edinburgh is best explained by someone wanting the birth away from Glasgow.

Part 4. Where the baby went

  • 1911 census, 16 Lochrin Place, Edinburgh: Elizabeth (Lizzie) Douglas, her daughter Janet, and three-year-old "Laurence B.," recorded as son. Lochrin Place is about 0.4 miles from the maternity hospital.
  • Lizzie's own history: born 31 May 1874 at Gilmerton to Ann McDonald, domestic servant. The entry is marked illegitimate and names no father. She was raised by her grandparents and put their names in the parents' column when she married Robert Douglas in 1899. She had lived the same path Laurence did.
  • The neighborhood: every address for Lizzie is within a mile of the hospital: 4 Hatton Place (1899), 5 Beaumont Place (1901), 16 Lochrin Place (1911).

Part 5. The witness

Laurence's daughter has always understood that the father was a doctor and that the gardener was connected. That account came first. The records then turned up a doctor whose uncle owned the estate where Agnes lived, and a gardener on that estate whose family had known her since childhood. When family memory predicts what the documents later show, it deserves weight.

Part 6. The theory of the case

  1. Binnie, a young unmarried doctor in Glasgow, had access to his uncle's country house.
  2. Agnes, a young servant whose life centred on Shieldhill, became pregnant around September 1906.
  3. The gardener's family, who worked for the Dunlops and had known Agnes all her life, kept her close through the pregnancy.
  4. She gave birth in Edinburgh rather than Glasgow, away from the doctor's city.
  5. The baby went to Lizzie, a woman near the hospital who knew this path from her own childhood.
  6. Within a year, the doctor had left Scotland.

Part 7. What the defense would say

  1. No record puts Binnie at Shieldhill or near Agnes. This is the biggest gap.
  2. Estate gardeners lived on the estate. That was his job. The census gives the address as Shieldhill, not which building.
  3. Agnes was the Archibalds' neighbor for 16 years. That Mary supervised the pregnancy is a reasonable guess, but no record says it.
  4. Other men were at Shieldhill: the knight, staff, visitors, men in the village. The records name none of them either way.
  5. Edinburgh has innocent explanations: a large teaching hospital that took unmarried women, distance from Agnes's own community, or a referral by a church, employer or midwife.
  6. Doctors moved to England for jobs and marriages.
  7. The family account is secondhand and over a century old. "A doctor" could describe many men.
  8. The "B" in "Laurence B." most likely stands for Brown, his mother's surname.
  9. No record yet shows how Agnes's side connected to Lizzie's side.

Part 8. Assessment

This is a circumstantial case for further investigation, not proof. The doctor fits, the family memory fits, the estate fits and the geography fits. The most important link, Binnie and Agnes in the same place, is not yet in any record.

Part 9. Records that could settle it

  1. DNA: an autosomal test from Laurence's daughter, his closest living relative, compared against descendants of the Dunlop and Binnie families.
  2. Royal Maternity Hospital register, June 1907 (Lothian Health Services Archive): referral, prior address, and who received the baby.
  3. A paternity decree: a sheriff-court action for support may appear as a Register of Corrected Entries note on Laurence's birth entry.
  4. Poor law records: parish relief files often named the alleged father.
  5. Kirk session minutes, Libberton and Quothquan: churches often recorded the man's name.
  6. Shieldhill estate papers and the local press, 1906: guests, family visits, Binnie's movements.
  7. Binnie's letters to Marie Stopes, 1916–1945, British Library Add MS 58564.

Conclusion: the evidence justifies a full investigation. It does not yet justify a conclusion.
