Death of Professor James Dunlop.

Our readers will learn with much regret of the death of Professor James Dunlop, M.D., which took place at his residence, Carlton Place, Glasgow, on Wednesday morning. Dr Dunlop had been in failing health for some years, and latterly it had been apparent to his friends that the weakness from which he suffered was gradually increasing. Last week unfavourable symptoms appeared, but immediate danger was not apprehended, and the end came somewhat suddenly. Dr Dunlop, who was 67 years of age, was a native of Campbeltown. He was educated at the Grammar School here, and about half a century ago, with his brothers Archibald, Nathaniel, and Matthew, went to Glasgow. While the three elder brothers went into business—in which each in his own sphere became well known—James entered Glasgow University, then in the old building in the High Street. There he had a distinguished career, carrying off numerous prizes. In 1857 he took the degree of M.D., and a year later became a Licentiate of the Faculty of Physicians and Surgeons. Immediately on graduation he received an appointment as one of the assistants in the Royal Infirmary, where he had the advantage of working under Dr Joseph, now Lord, Lister. The special bent of his studies had been in the direction of surgery, and a vacancy occurring in Anderson's College through the transference of Sir George MacLeod to the Chair of Surgery in the University, notwithstanding his youth and comparatively limited experience Dr Dunlop became a candidate. He was well supported by Dr Lister and others, who knew and could appreciate his ability, and was duly elected to the professorship, holding the office for about twenty years. His appointment was continued as one of the surgeons of the Royal Infirmary, with which his connection extended over a period of forty years. Throughout the profession he was recognised as an accomplished surgeon, and as a lecturer he was successful and highly popular with a long succession of students. Dr Dunlop took an active part in the management of various other medical institutions in the city. He held the office of surgeon of the Lock Hospital, he was governor of the Samaritan Hospital, and, one of the promoters of the Victoria Hospital, he continued to be one of the governors up till the time of his death. Among other appointments which he held was that of Medico-Legal Adviser to the Crown, and in this connection his name has been associated with all the great criminal trials which have taken place in the city for many years. He had a thorough knowledge of medical jurisprudence, and made an admirable witness, and great reliance was placed upon his judgment by some of the most eminent of our criminal judges. He also held an appointment as a consulting surgeon to the Caledonian Railway Company, and to the Clyde Pilot Board. With other medical men Dr Dunlop took an active part in the movement which led to the formation of the sanitary department of the city, under Sir Wm. Gairdner, and on the occasion of a former epidemic of small-pox he was an earnest advocate of a policy of systematic vaccination and isolation, which led to the stamping out of the scourge. From the commencement of the volunteer movement Dr Dunlop was one of its most ardent supporters, and he was a member of the bodyguard of Queen Victoria on the occasion of the opening of the water-works at Loch Katrine. He joined originally as a private in the University Corps, but his services were subsequently transferred to the Southern Division, and he has been for many years connected with the 3rd L.R.V. His first commission as Lieutenant was dated 24th November, 1860; he became Honorary Lieutenant-Colonel on 1st February, 1889; and in the following year he was appointed to the position of Brigade-Surgeon-Lieutenant-Colonel to the Glasgow Brigade. But Dr Dunlop's interest in all branches of military medical work began before his connection with the volunteer movement, for at the time of the Crimean War he volunteered for the front. He was too young, however, at the time, and the offer was declined. Despite his failing health and advancing years his ardour and enthusiasm continued to the end, and on the outbreak of the South African War he again volunteered for service in the field. As he himself said with characteristic humour, he was then told he was too old. Nevertheless his services were accepted, and in the beginning of January of last year he sailed for South Africa as acting surgeon on board the troopship "Bavaria." On his arrival at Cape Town he was placed by the medical authorities in charge of several of the surgical wards in the Wynberg Base Hospital, and there he did splendid work at a time of great pressure upon this department of the army. He remained at Wynberg between six and seven months, devoting all his skill to the treatment of Briton and Boer alike. On the eve of his departure he was examined as a witness before the Medical Commission, and from his long professional experience was able to give evidence of great value in the elucidation of the charges the Commissioners were appointed to investigate. Dr Dunlop's second son, it is worthy of note, is at the present moment serving as a civil surgeon with the army in South Africa. On the return of Dr Dunlop he was entertained at a banquet by his comrades in the 3rd L.R.V. and other friends, and at that gathering he gave a lively and interesting account of his experiences. "My residence with the colours and under the British flag in South Africa has (he then said) been a bright page in my life's history, a history which began in the year 1849—or rather when I began my medical studies. My residence in Africa seems to me a fitting, an opportune, and, I might almost say without egotism, a graceful finish to what has been a fairly successful professional life. Some of you may remember that at this time last year it was publicly noted that in the year 1854 I had volunteered for service in the Crimea. At that time I was declared to be rather young, and at this time last year—I think it was about the 10th of October, this day twelve months ago—the ultimatum having been sent on the previous day, I indicated to the War Office that I was quite prepared to go out to Africa to offer our wounded soldiers the benefit of long professional experience. I was declared, however, to be rather old, so that really the War Office in the beginning and the ending had, I think, made a gigantic blunder. When I appeared in the War Office, as I did on the 4th or 5th of January, and saluted the D.G., I said—"Director-General, would you be pleased to look at me; can you find that there is a surgeon of 67 years of age in the British army so active, so juvenile, and sharp in intellect as your friend with whom you used to smoke when we were lads together in the Glasgow Royal Infirmary?" I may say, gentlemen, he smiled. I mentioned to him that I had not, in calling upon him, the slightest intention of asking him to send me out to Africa. I said, 'I am being sent out to Africa by the regiment; I am going out as a volunteer medical officer—the senior medical officer of the Third Lanark Rifle Volunteers. I am going out at my own cost; I want no money. I shall pay my own way out; I shall pay my way while I am there, and I shall pay my way back. I want no money from the Government. I simply at the close of my life wish to render whatever service I may be able to do in Southern Africa.' Really, gentlemen, there was nothing to be said; the thing was closed. A few days thereafter I was on board the 'Bavaria,' a troopship carrying 2,200 soldiers—fighting men—in addition to some 60 or 70 officers—regimental officers—officers going out to do special work in Southern Africa, and there were also six or seven—I think probably six would be the number—of Army Medical Officers, with orderlies and ambulance men, going out, in fact, to succour the wounded at the front." After narrating his experiences, interspersed with numerous anecdotes of a characteristic kind, he closed by saying, in words prophetic—"Now, gentlemen, in conclusion, I thank you very much for your reception of me. I think that my mission to South Africa may be accepted as a fitting conclusion to a busy professional life, and I think it will close my relationship with the Third Lanark Rifle Volunteers. My years are up. I have to retire, gentlemen, and this may be my last appearance here as an officer of the regiment, but you may rest assured that as long as I have life, and so long as I have the gift of speech, I will always be able to say something on behalf of the Third Lanark to which I have been attached for so many years. I am the oldest, and I may say the only, commissioned officer now connected with the regiment that was in it when it was first formed, and I think the time is come when I will have to retire, and I can only say that on going into retirement I will carry away with me the most kindly feelings towards every soldier and every officer connected with the 3rd L.R.V." Owing to failing health Dr Dunlop relinquished his practice some years ago, and since that time, save for his short period of service in South Africa, he principally concerned himself with the duties of the management of the various hospitals and other institutions with which he was connected. One of his favourite pursuits, to which much of his leisure time and his holidays throughout his life were devoted, was the study of natural history; and on the subjects of fishes and birds he was a recognised authority. For a number of years he contributed to the Glasgow Herald a series of letters descriptive of his holiday at Carradale, in which valuable additions were made to the natural history of the district. His interest in his favourite subject continued up to the time of his death, and only a few days ago there arrived from South Africa a collection of fishes and other objects which he had gathered together during his stay in the colony. In private life Dr Dunlop was one of the most estimable of men—genial, kindly, and with a rich fund of humour, and he gathered around him a wide circle of friends. He is survived by a widow, six sons, and two daughters. The eldest son is in business in London, the second, as has been stated, is in service as a surgeon in South Africa, and the third a barrister in London.

What the record says

His hometown paper calls him Professor James Dunlop. He died at his house in Carlton Place, Glasgow, on Wednesday morning, March 13, 1901, aged 67, after some years of failing health.

He was born in Campbeltown and went to its Grammar School. About fifty years earlier he went to Glasgow “with his brothers Archibald, Nathaniel, and Matthew.” The three older brothers went into business; James entered Glasgow University, took his M.D. in 1857, and worked at the Royal Infirmary under Joseph Lister.

He held the professorship of surgery at Anderson’s College for about twenty years, was a surgeon of the Royal Infirmary for forty years, and served as Medico-Legal Adviser to the Crown in the great criminal trials in Glasgow.

At 67 he volunteered for the South African War, sailed in January 1900 as acting surgeon on the troopship Bavaria at his own expense, and worked six to seven months in the surgical wards of the Wynberg Base Hospital.

It says his second son (Binnie) was then serving as a civil surgeon with the army in South Africa. His eldest son was in business in London and his third son a barrister in London. He left a widow, six sons and two daughters.

Why it matters

It names Nathaniel as James’s brother in a newspaper of the time. The family count matches the Glasgow Medical Journal obituary (Item 12), so that part is confirmation, not a new fact. The details of his South African service and his sons’ occupations are new to the project.
