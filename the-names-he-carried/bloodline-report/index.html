Bloodline Report: Laurence’s Mother’s Line Traced Back to 1479

Research notes, not part of the book · every claim marked by how sure we are

This morning, Laurence Brown Douglas’s family tree on his mother’s side stopped at his great-grandparents, a ploughman and his wife in the Peeblesshire hills. By the end of the day it reached back fourteen generations, to a Galloway laird born in 1479 who read a forbidden English New Testament to his tenants in a wood. In between are an innkeeper and his donkey-drawn mail cart, a provost of Dumfries, a judge of the wills court, a Covenanter shot on his way to battle, his son imprisoned on the Bass Rock, and the Lord Advocate who gave the National Covenant of 1638 its legal shape.

The headlines

  • Back to 1479. The line now reaches Alexander Gordon of Airds (1479–1580), Laurence’s twelfth great-grandfather and one of the first reformers in Galloway.
  • The Covenant. Sir Thomas Hope (1573–1646), Lord Advocate under Charles I and the legal mind behind the National Covenant, is his tenth great-grandfather.
  • The Covenanters. Three generations of Gordons of Earlstoun stood with the Covenant. William was shot by dragoons on the way to Bothwell Bridge in 1679, and his son Sir Alexander fought there, fled to Holland, and spent six years in prison, including on the Bass Rock.
  • A provost. James Corrie of Speddoch, his sixth great-grandfather, governed Dumfries in the 1710s, 1720s and 1730s.
  • A judge. John Goldie of Craigmuie, his fifth great-grandfather, was Commissary of Dumfriesshire for more than forty years.
  • A Bank of England question. Jean Paterson, his seventh great-grandmother, was called a near relative of William Paterson, founder of the Bank of England. She was not his sister. A cousin is possible, but not proven.
  • Deeper still. The Murray line reaches a laird who died in 1413, and the Chalmers pedigree reaches a royal chamberlain of the 1100s. Both come from published genealogies, not proven records.
  • Home ground. The Murrays of Blackbarony, his tenth great-grandparents, were lairds in Peeblesshire, the same county where his Brown and McGarth ancestors worked the farms two hundred years later.
  • An inn and a donkey. His great-great-grandparents kept the Beild at Tweedsmuir, and his great-great-grandfather carried the mail in a cart called the Chariot, pulled by a donkey named Omar Pasha.

The line at a glance

Each step below is one generation further back from Laurence.

  1. Mother: Agnes McGarth Brown (born 1885, Edston, Peeblesshire)
  2. Grandfather: George Brown (1862–1886, ploughman)
  3. Great-grandmother: Agnes McGarth (1840–1924, born at the Beild, Tweedsmuir)
  4. Great-great-grandparents: Tweedie McGarth and Jean Goldie (kept the Beild inn, Tweedsmuir)
  5. Third great-grandparents: Alexander Goldie and Agnes Mundell (farmers in Kirkmichael, Tinwald and Wamphray)
  6. Fourth great-grandfather: James Goldie (1737–1763, factor to the Duke of Queensberry)
  7. Fifth great-grandparents: John Goldie of Craigmuie and Jean Corrie (the Commissary judge of Dumfries and the provost’s daughter)
  8. Sixth great-grandparents: Edward Goldie and Mary Gordon; James Corrie of Speddoch and Agnes McKitrick (laird of Craigmuie; the Covenanter’s daughter; the provost of Dumfries)
  9. Seventh great-grandparents: Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun and Janet Hamilton; William Corrie and Jean Paterson; James McKitrick (Covenanter on the Bass Rock; bailie of Dumfries; merchant)
  10. Eighth great-grandparents: William Gordon of Earlstoun and Mary Hope; Sir Thomas Hamilton of Preston (Covenanter shot on the way to Bothwell Bridge; East Lothian laird)
  11. Ninth great-grandparents: Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun and Elizabeth Gordon; Sir John Hope, Lord Craighall, and Margaret Murray (Covenanting elder of 1638; judge of the Court of Session)
  12. Tenth great-grandparents: John Gordon of Airds and Mary Chalmers; John Gordon of Penninghame; Sir Thomas Hope, Lord Advocate, and Elizabeth Bennet; Sir Archibald Murray of Blackbarony and Margaret Maule (Galloway and Peeblesshire lairds; the lawyer behind the National Covenant)
  13. Eleventh great-grandparents: James Chalmers of Gadgirth; Henry Hope and Jacqueline de Tott; Sir John Murray of Blackbarony and Margaret Hamilton (Ayrshire reformers; an Edinburgh merchant and his French wife)
  14. Twelfth great-grandfather: Alexander Gordon of Airds (1479–1580, early reformer of Galloway)

How the trail was followed

The key was a name. Laurence’s mother was registered as Agnes McGarth Brown, and her father, George Brown, gave her his own mother’s name. George’s death register named his parents, William Brown and Agnes McGarth. Agnes McGarth’s baptism at Tweedsmuir named her parents, Tweedie McGarth and Jean Goldie. Jean Goldie’s baptism at Wamphray named hers, Alexander Goldie and Agnes Mundell.

From there a family tree on Ancestry, built by another descendant (PamelaWills909’s Cairns Family Tree), carried the Goldies back through Kirkmichael to the Goldies of Craigmuie and the Corries of Dumfries. The Goldie gravestone in Dumfries and the 1899 Records of the Corrie Family confirmed those families. Mary Gordon’s gravestone at Balmaghie, naming her as a daughter of Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun, opened the door to the published histories of the Gordons, Hopes, Hamiltons and Murrays: the Dictionary of National Biography, Burke’s Peerage and their modern copies.

The Tweedsmuir years

Tweedie McGarth (1811–1876) and Jean Goldie (1808–1891) kept the Beild, an old coaching inn on the Edinburgh–Dumfries road below Oliver Dod. Tweedie was innkeeper, shopkeeper, and from 1847 the Post Office messenger between Broughton and Tweedsmuir. His wife’s obituary remembers his home-made mail cart and his donkey. Jean ran the Beild for more than fifty years and died there on Christmas Eve 1891. Their daughter Agnes, born at the Beild in 1840, married William Brown, a ploughman, in 1858. Their son George was Laurence’s grandfather. New today: photographs of the Beild and of the couple’s gravestone at Tweedsmuir Kirk, Jean’s full obituary, and the couple’s twelve children.

Down into Dumfriesshire

Jean Goldie was born at Wamphray in 1808, the daughter of Alexander Goldie (1762–1822), who farmed in Kirkmichael, Tinwald and Wamphray, and Agnes Mundell of Cleughbrae, Mouswald. Alexander was baptised at Kirkmichael in 1762. A tree on Ancestry makes him the son of James Goldie (1737–1763), the eldest son of the Goldies of Craigmuie. James was made factor to the Duke of Queensberry in 1761 and died two years later at 26, so the family estate passed to his younger brother. That would explain why Laurence’s ancestors after him were working farmers. This is the weakest link in the chain, and everything further back depends on it.

The judge and the provost

John Goldie of Craigmuie (1708–1776) was Commissary of Dumfriesshire, the judge of wills and estates, from 1734 for more than forty years. He married Jean Corrie, the daughter of James Corrie of Speddoch (1684–1742), a merchant who was Provost of Dumfries for four terms between 1718 and 1736. After his first wife, Agnes McKitrick, died, the provost married John Goldie’s sister Janet, so the two families were tied together twice. Their gravestone in Dumfries still names them: “JOHN GOLDIE, Esq. of Craigmuie and Commissary of Dfrs. … JEAN CORRIE, his wife, daughter of JAMES CORRIE, Esq. of Speddoch … JAMES, their eldest son, born 1737, died 1763.”

The Covenanters of Earlstoun

John Goldie’s mother, Mary Gordon (1681–1723), was a daughter of Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun (1650–1726) and Janet Hamilton. Sir Alexander was a Covenanter, one of the Presbyterians who resisted the Crown’s control of the Church. He fought at Bothwell Bridge in 1679, fled to Holland, and was sentenced to death. Captured in 1683, he was spared, and held for six years in Edinburgh Castle, on the Bass Rock and in Blackness Castle until the Revolution freed him in 1689. Janet Hamilton’s brother, Sir Robert Hamilton of Preston, was one of the Covenanter commanders.

Sir Alexander’s father, William Gordon of Earlstoun (1614–1679), was riding to join the Covenanter army at Bothwell Bridge when English dragoons shot him. His grandfather, Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun (1587–1654), sat as an elder at the Glasgow General Assembly of 1638, where the Covenant was first put into action. And the family’s Protestant roots go back to Alexander Gordon of Airds (1479–1580), who read Wycliffe’s New Testament aloud to his tenants in the wood of Airds, decades before Scotland broke with Rome. Mary Gordon’s second husband was the Rev. John Macmillan, the Covenanting minister of Balmaghie. Her gravestone there begins: “Here ly the Remains of Mistress May Gordon, daughter of Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun.”

The lawyer behind the Covenant

William Gordon’s wife, Mary Hope, was a daughter of Sir John Hope, Lord Craighall (about 1605–1654), a judge of the Court of Session, and Margaret Murray of Blackbarony. Sir John’s father was Sir Thomas Hope of Craighall (1573–1646), Lord Advocate under Charles I and the legal mind behind the National Covenant of 1638. He made sure its wording was not treason. His parents were Henry Hope, an Edinburgh merchant who traded with France and Holland, and Jacqueline de Tott, a Frenchwoman. So the man who gave the Covenant its legal shape was the great-great-grandfather of Mary Gordon, whose own father and grandfather suffered for it.

Deeper still: the Murrays and the Chalmers

The Murrays of Blackbarony. Margaret Murray’s grandfather, Sir John Murray of Blackbarony (died 1609), sat in Parliament for Peeblesshire and married Margaret Hamilton of Innerwick. His parents were Sir Andrew Murray of Blackbarony (died 1572) and Grizel Bethune of Creich. By her first marriage, Grizel was the mother of Sir Walter Scott, 4th of Buccleuch, an ancestor of the Dukes of Buccleuch. Andrew’s father, John Murray, died on September 9, 1513, the day of the Battle of Flodden. Genealogies carry the line back through the Murrays of Falahill to a Patrick Murray who died in 1413, Laurence’s sixteenth great-grandfather.

The Chalmers of Gadgirth. Mary Chalmers was a daughter of James Chalmers of Gadgirth (died after 1588) and Marion Fullerton of Dreghorn. Her grandfather, James Chalmers (living 1550), who married Annabella Cunningham of Caprington, was probably the reformer John Knox called one of the boldest leaders of the Reformation. The family pedigree runs back through a marriage about 1440 to Elizabeth Hamilton, sister of the first Lord Hamilton, and a laird killed at the Battle of the Herrings in 1429, to Herbert de Camera, chamberlain to King David I in the 1100s. Some of those middle generations are disputed.

The Bank of England question

James Corrie’s mother was Jean Paterson. A 19th-century book calls her the sister of William Paterson (1658–1719), the Dumfriesshire man who founded the Bank of England. The records don’t support that. Paterson’s 1718 will names his only surviving sister as Elizabeth, and an 1865 study of his family names his sisters as Janet and Elizabeth. The Corrie family’s own historian, writing in 1899, called Jean “a near relative” of Paterson but could not say how. One real link survives: Paterson owed Jean’s husband, William Corrie, £45. The verdict for now is a possible cousin, not proven.

What is solid, and what is not

  • Solid: Laurence’s mother back to Tweedie McGarth and Jean Goldie (civil and parish registers, gravestone).
  • Solid: Jean Goldie’s parents, Alexander Goldie and Agnes Mundell (Wamphray baptism register).
  • Needs checking: whether Alexander Goldie (born 1762) was the son of James Goldie of Craigmuie. Everything from the Goldies of Craigmuie back to 1479 depends on this one link.
  • Solid, on that condition: the Goldies of Craigmuie, the Corries of Speddoch, and Mary Gordon as Sir Alexander Gordon’s daughter (gravestones and Records of the Corrie Family, 1899).
  • Published, not yet checked against original records: the Gordon, Hope, Hamilton, Murray and Chalmers generations, from the Dictionary of National Biography, Burke’s Peerage, The Peerage, Stirnet and Wikipedia. These are well-known families, and their pedigrees have been printed for 150 years, but printed pedigrees can be wrong.
  • Published tradition only: the Murrays of Falahill before 1513 and the Chalmers of Gadgirth before 1550.
  • Open: Margaret Maule’s parents; the two Gordon generations between Alexander Gordon of Airds and John Gordon of Airds and Earlstoun; which James Chalmers of Gadgirth was Mary Chalmers’s father; Sir Thomas Hamilton of Preston’s parents and which of his wives was Janet’s mother; Jean Paterson’s link to William Paterson; the parents of William Corrie, Michael McGarth and Margaret Larkin.

Corrections made today

  • The Ancestry tree puts Kirkmichael in Ayrshire. This Kirkmichael is in Dumfriesshire.
  • The tree shows Alexander Goldie and Agnes Mundell marrying in 1871, decades after both had died.
  • Find a Grave names Jean Goldie’s father as William. Her baptism names him Alexander.
  • The tree gives James Goldie children born after his death in 1763. They belong to a different James Goldie.
  • A 19th-century book calls Jean Paterson William Paterson’s sister. She was not.
  • FamilySearch places Sir Alexander Gordon’s birth in Berwickshire and his death at Oban. Earlstoun is in Galloway.
  • Online trees give Sir Alexander a daughter Anne born in 1670, six years before his marriage.
