The Provost, the Judge and the Laird: Laurence’s Dumfries Ancestors

Research notes, not part of the book · what we know, and what we don’t

Laurence Brown Douglas was born in Edinburgh in 1907 as Lawrence Brown, the son of Agnes Brown, a young woman from a family of Peeblesshire ploughmen. Go back eight generations on his mother’s side, though, and the family looks very different. In the early 1700s his ancestors in Dumfries included a provost of the town, the judge of its wills court, and the daughter of a Galloway laird. One branch may even be related to the man who founded the Bank of England.

How Laurence connects

The line runs through his mother’s father. Laurence’s mother was Agnes McGarth Brown, born in 1885. Her father, George Brown, was the son of Agnes McGarth of the Beild at Tweedsmuir. Agnes McGarth’s mother, Jean Goldie, was born at Wamphray in Dumfriesshire in 1808. From Jean the line climbs straight back into Dumfries:

  1. Jean Goldie (1808–1891), Laurence’s great-great-grandmother
  2. Alexander Goldie (1762–1822), a farmer in Kirkmichael, Tinwald and Wamphray, his third great-grandfather
  3. James Goldie (1737–1763), his fourth great-grandfather
  4. John Goldie of Craigmuie, Commissary of Dumfries, and Jean Corrie, his fifth great-grandparents
  5. Edward Goldie of Craigmuie and Mary Gordon, and James Corrie of Speddoch and Agnes McKitrick, his sixth great-grandparents
  6. Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun and Janet Hamilton, William Corrie and Jean Paterson, and James McKitrick, merchant, his seventh great-grandparents

For Laurence’s daughter, Doug’s mother, each of these is one generation further back. For Doug, it is two.

The judge: John Goldie of Craigmuie

The Goldies came from England to Closeburn in Dumfriesshire during the civil wars of the 1600s. An Edward Goldie became tenant of Craigmuie in 1676 and bought it in 1679. His son Edward, the second of Craigmuie, married Mary Gordon in 1701 and died in 1711, when his son John was three.

That son, John Goldie (1708–1776), became Commissary of Dumfriesshire in 1734 and held the post for more than forty years. The commissary court dealt with wills, estates and marriages, so for four decades the settling of family property in the district passed through his hands. The National Archives at Kew holds a commission appointing a John Goldie Commissary Clerk of Dumfries. Papers of the Goldie family of Craigmuie, including letters to John, survive at Dumfries and Galloway Archives.

John married Jean Corrie in August 1735. Their gravestone at Dumfries still names them: John Goldie, Esq. of Craigmuie, Commissary; Jean Corrie, “daughter of James Corrie, Esq. of Speddoch”; and “James, their eldest son, born 1737, died 1763.”

The young factor: James Goldie

James, the eldest son, should have inherited Craigmuie. In 1761 he was appointed chamberlain and factor to the Duke of Queensberry, managing one of the largest estates in the south of Scotland. A family history says he was among the first in the district to farm by science. He died in 1763, aged 26, and the estate passed to his younger brother Thomas. That is how this line lost Craigmuie, and why Laurence’s ancestors after him were working farmers, not lairds.

The provost: James Corrie of Speddoch

Jean Corrie’s father, James Corrie (1684–1742), was a Dumfries merchant who became Provost of Dumfries in 1718–20 and again for three more terms, the last ending in 1736. He bought the estate of Speddoch, in Holywood parish about ten miles from the town. His first wife, Agnes McKitrick, a merchant’s daughter, died in 1721 at 31, when Jean was nine. He then married Janet Goldie, the sister of the John Goldie who later married his daughter. The two families were tied together twice over.

The laird’s daughter: Mary Gordon of Earlstoun

Edward Goldie’s wife, Mary Gordon, was the second daughter of Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun and Janet Hamilton, of Dalry in Galloway. After Edward died she married the Rev. John Macmillan, the Covenanting minister of Balmaghie. Her gravestone at Balmaghie begins, “Here ly the Remains of Mistress May Gordon, daughter of Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun.” She died in 1723, aged 43. In 1843 two of her great-great-grandsons had the stone renewed.

The Bank of England question

James Corrie’s mother was Jean Paterson. A 19th-century book, The Scottish Nation, calls her the sister of William Paterson (1658–1719), the Dumfriesshire man who founded the Bank of England and planned the Darien scheme. The evidence does not support that. William Paterson’s 1718 will names his only surviving sister as Elizabeth, and a study of his family published in 1865 names his sisters as Janet and Elizabeth, not Jean. The Corrie family’s own historian, writing in 1899, called Jean “a near relative” of Paterson. She thought a connection very probable but admitted she could not say what it was. The claim goes back to a family letter written in 1800. One hard link survives: the testament of Jean’s son Samuel records that William Paterson owed her husband, William Corrie, £45. The likeliest answer is that she was a cousin, but that is not proven.

What we know

  • The Goldie gravestone at Dumfries names John Goldie of Craigmuie, Commissary; Jean Corrie, daughter of James Corrie of Speddoch; and James, their eldest son, 1737–1763.
  • Mary Gordon’s gravestone at Balmaghie names her as the daughter of Sir Alexander Gordon of Earlstoun and the wife of Edward Goldie of Craigmuie.
  • Records of the Corrie Family (1899) gives the Corrie and Goldie generations, the provost’s terms, and the double marriage between the families.
  • Records on Ancestry give Alexander Goldie’s 1762 baptism at Kirkmichael and his family with Agnes Mundell. Their daughter Jean Goldie’s line runs down to Laurence through the McGarths of Tweedsmuir.

What we don’t know yet

  • The weakest link: whether Alexander Goldie, born at Kirkmichael in 1762, was really the son of James Goldie of Craigmuie. An Ancestry tree says so and names his mother as Ann Wilson. The 1899 family history doesn’t name a wife for James at all. The Kirkmichael baptism register for 1762 should settle it.
  • Whether Jean Paterson was related to William Paterson of the Bank of England, and how.
  • Who William Corrie’s own parents were. His family’s historian could not find them.
  • The dates in the Ancestry tree for the earliest people (William Corrie, Jean Paterson, James McKitrick), which still need checking against the Dumfries parish registers.
  • What the Goldie of Craigmuie papers at Dumfries and Galloway Archives contain.
