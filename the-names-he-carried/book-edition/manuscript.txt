The Names He Carried

The Laurence Brown Douglas Story

Prologue. The Watch

Providence, R.I., March 9, 1961

I am sitting here with an antique pocket watch in my hand. It was my grandfather's.

He died in 1961, five years before I was born, and the watch stayed with my grandmother, whom everyone called Nan. One day she set it in my hand and whispered, “This belonged to your grandfather. He brought it over from Scotland. You have it now.” Inside the case, engraved in the metal, was a single word: Edinburgh.

When I was a little boy, Nan would take me to McDonald’s in her dark brown 1960s Valiant, and our talk always found its way back to Laurence. She would smile and tell me how much I reminded her of him, and that I was named after him. She spoke of him with warmth and a quiet sadness, and she never remarried. But she never told me where in Scotland he came from, or why he left.

So I went looking for him, through ships’ manifests and city directories, until I could bring him back to life on the page.

When he died, at fifty-three, his obituary said he was born in Edinburgh to Robert and Elizabeth Douglas. It did not say that his birth was recorded under another name, to another mother. On the day he was born, he was Lawrence Brown.

This is the story of how he came by the names he carried.

Part One: Clydesdale

Chapter 1. The Plowmen

Peeblesshire, Scotland, 1835–1871

On census night in 1911 he was three years old, and he was the son of the house.

He lived with Lizzie Douglas and her daughter Janet in an apartment at Tollcross, in Edinburgh, and Robert Douglas was the man who would raise him. A year later the family crossed the Forth to Dunfermline, and he went with them, to a new flat, a new school and the big library up the hill. He was an ordinary, happy boy, and he followed the man and woman who were raising him the way any small boy follows his father and mother. They were the only parents he would ever remember, and fifty years later, when he died in Rhode Island, his obituary named them as his mother and father.

But there was another family behind him, with other names, and a watch with one word inside it. To find them you have to go back to the hills of the Scottish Borders, half a century before he was born, to a family of plowmen.

The river Tweed rises high in the hills of Peeblesshire, near the great green hollow called the Devil's Beef Tub, and runs east past Tweedsmuir, Stobo and Lyne toward the market town of Peebles. It is high, bare sheep country, and the people who worked it were as hardy as the land.

Agnes McGarth was born at Tweedsmuir around 1840. The McGarths were hill people. In 1851 the census found her father, Tweedie McGarth, and her mother, Jane, a woman from Wamphray in Dumfriesshire, with a house full of children: Jane, Agnes, James Tweedie, Sarah, Ann and Janet. Their names would outlast them. "Tweedie" and "McGarth" were handed down as middle names for three generations, a way of remembering who you came from, and the proudest thing a family could leave.

Agnes married William Brown, a plowman. He walked behind a pair of horses in all weathers, turning the stony ground for oats and turnips, and he was good at it. A plowman's wages came partly in money and partly in oatmeal, potatoes, milk and coal, and his cottage came with the job. When the year's work ended at the term, a plowman's family loaded its furniture onto a farm cart, set the children up on top, and rolled on to the next farm.

Who the plowmen were

In the Borders the skilled plowman was called a hind. He was a family man, hired for a whole year at a time, usually in March or at Whitsunday, and paid partly in cash and partly in kind, with a cottage and a garden for his household.

Hiring was done face to face at the fairs. Men stood in the market and the farmers came round to judge their strength and skill and strike a bargain. At the Carnwath fair, a few miles from Quothquan, a good plowman in 1825 earned six or seven pounds for the half year, with his house, meal and potatoes, his coal carted at no charge, food at harvest and sometimes milk. By 1862 the best men earned twelve pounds or more for the half year.

At the end of the term a family might load its furniture on a cart and move to the next farm. It was hard, honest work, and it fed a household.

William and Agnes raised a big family, ten children or more. Two of their boys matter to this story.

Lawrence Tweedie Brown was born on September 14, 1860, in Peebles. George was born on July 18, 1862. In 1871 the census found them together under their father's roof, Lawrence ten and George eight, two boys who had grown up walking the furrows behind their father.

Lawrence would marry Isabella Graham from Cambusnethan, raise a family at Traquair and Sanquhar, and settle at last in Quebec. He died in 1943. Forty-seven years after his own birth, his niece would give his name to her son.

George stayed with the land. He followed his father behind the plow, and at twenty he married Christina Henderson at Quothquan, in the upper valley of the Clyde. They had two little daughters. But George did not live to see them grown.

One of those daughters was Agnes. She would give her son her uncle's name, Lawrence, and before he was four years old he would carry another.

Chapter 2. Agnes

Edston, Peebles, 1883–1886

She was born in a plowman's cottage a week before Christmas, in the dark of a Border winter, and before the snow was off the hills she had lost her father.

Her story starts two and a half years earlier, on a summer day in the upper valley of the Clyde.

On Friday, June 1, 1883, at Quothquan, in the parish of Libberton, George Brown married Christina Henderson. He was twenty, a young plowman with strong hands and his whole life in front of him. She was twenty-three, the eldest daughter of Alexander Henderson and Jane Robb, a Libberton family through and through. Her people lived under Quothquan Law, a bald round hill above the river, four miles from the market town of Biggar. Across the Clyde, filling the southwest sky, stood Tinto, the red hill of Clydesdale, with a great cairn on its summit that the old folk said had been carried up stone by stone. Christina had grown up with that hill in her window. So would her daughters.

That August their first child was born at Quothquan, a girl, and they named her Mary Henderson Brown. Christina's little brother Andrew, born when she was already sixteen, was hardly more than a boy himself. Years later he would matter a great deal to this family.

A plowman went where the work was, and by the winter of 1885 George had found a place across the county line, at Edston, a farm in the parish of Lyne, a few miles west of Peebles. It was a fresh start. The farm cottages there, low and stone-built under slate roofs, still stand along the road. The young family moved in: George, Christina and little Mary, not yet two and a half. Cottages like theirs had a coal fire in the grate and a box bed set into the wall, with the horses in the stable across the yard and the hills all around. They had steady work, a roof, each other, and a second child on the way.

At half past two in the afternoon on Friday, December 18, 1885, Christina gave birth to another girl.

On the last day of the year George went into Peebles to register her, and he signed the book himself. He named her for his own mother: Agnes McGarth Brown. It was his gift to her, the family name from the hills of Tweedsmuir.

The new year came in. The days began to lengthen. Out in the fields the ground was hardening toward the spring plowing, the busiest weeks of a plowman's year, and George was a strong young man of twenty-three who worked outdoors every day of the year.

Then, in March, with no warning, an illness took hold in his chest. It was pleuro-pneumonia, an infection of the lungs and the lining around them, and in 1886 no doctor anywhere had a medicine that could stop it. There would be no antibiotics for another half century. For three weeks he fought it in the cottage at Edston, with Christina and their two little daughters around him.

George Brown died at half past one in the morning on Monday, April 5, 1886. The register gives the causes as pleuro-pneumonia, of twenty-one days, and exhaustion. He was twenty-three years old. His baby daughter was three and a half months old. It was his own father, William Brown, the plowman, who went to the registrar to report his son's death.

Christina was a widow at twenty-six. The cottage at Edston went with the job, and the job had died with George. So she gathered up what they had and took her girls home to Libberton and her own people, a girl of two and a half at her side and a baby in her arms.

Agnes would never remember her father's face. Everything she knew of him she would learn from others. But she carried his mother's name all her life, and one day she would hand a name of his family on to a son of her own.

The valley under Tinto was where she would grow up. It was where her story would turn.

Chapter 3. The Cottages at Quothquan

Quothquan, Lanarkshire, 1891

The census of 1891 takes up the girls' story in their mother's village. Christina had died at Quothquan that February. Her name is on the Henderson family monument. When the census was taken on April 5, Mary was seven, Agnes five, and both were living with Christina's mother.

The census gave their address as Castle. A crofter, a mole catcher, a dressmaker, a stonebreaker and a farm servant were among the people recorded there. The census listed their grandmother, Jane Henderson, as fifty-six, born at Carnwath, and head of the household. Her daughter Jane was the dressmaker. Andrew was still at school.

Another young man lived with them. James Parker, eighteen and born at Biggar, was working as an agricultural laborer. Ten years earlier, when James was eight, the census had called him an adopted son. Mary and Agnes appear on the later return as Jane's granddaughters. Even on a census page, the differences in how the children had come into the house were visible.

A few entries away lived Ann Archibald, a widow. Her daughter was Mary Archibald. One son, Alexander, was a stonebreaker. The other, John, was working on a farm. Ten years later members of both families were still in the village, though in different households.

That spring and summer the two households lost people within days of one another. Jane Henderson died on June 8. John Archibald died on June 20, four days after his eighteenth birthday. The dates survive in the monumental inscriptions. By midsummer Agnes had lost her father, her mother and the grandmother in whose house the census had found her that April.

Chapter 4. The Knight of Shieldhill

Buckingham Palace, London, July 15, 1907

In the summer of 1907 the same place name appeared in two very different records. Agnes gave Shieldhill, Biggar, as part of her address when she registered her son. Later that month it appeared in the London Gazette beside the name of a new knight.

Nathaniel Dunlop had been born at Campbeltown. In 1847 he entered the Glasgow counting house of George Gillespie, whose business handled work for the Allan shipping line. In 1853 Dunlop joined the Allan firm itself. His career followed a route familiar to generations of Scottish emigrants, from Glasgow across the Atlantic to Canada. He came to run the shipping business and to chair the Clyde Navigation Trust.

The estate at Shieldhill, long held by the Chancellor family, came into his possession in 1898. The mansion incorporated an old stone tower. Its farms and cottages extended the estate far beyond the house itself.

On July 15, 1907, Edward the Seventh conferred the knighthood on Dunlop at Buckingham Palace. Among the other men knighted there that day were the painter Hubert von Herkomer, James Donaldson of the University of St. Andrews, and William Schwenck Gilbert, who had written the words to The Mikado. The Gazette published their names on July 26.

The honour belonged to Sir Nathaniel. The address belonged to a place where a great many people made their living. To follow Agnes, we have to leave the list of honours and look at the houses below his.

Chapter 5. Shieldhill

Quothquan, Lanarkshire, 1898–1906

By March 1901, the enumerator at Quothquan had names for the cottages. At Gean Tree lived Mary Archibald, a sewing mistress. Another household at Gean Tree consisted of James Grossart, an elderly road worker, and his granddaughter Marion. At Lime Tree Cottage the head of the house was Andrew Henderson.

Andrew was twenty-five and a groom. Local people later remembered him as a poet and a keeper of bees. His nieces were still together. Mary Brown was seventeen and Agnes was fifteen. James Parker, who had lived with their grandmother, appears again, this time as a boarder. He had become a gamekeeper.

The names tell us something about the village that an estate address alone cannot. Gean is a Scots name for the wild cherry. Lime Tree and Gean Tree were distinct addresses. A later history of the parish says Lime Tree Cottage became part of the house known as Geantree. The row once called Castle is known today as Huntfield Place. The census gives the order in which the houses were recorded. It would take more than the numbers on that page to say who shared a wall.

Mary Archibald's brother Alexander was now a domestic gardener. The census gave his address, and his wife Annie's, as Shieldhill. In August 1903 James Parker married Janet Crawford Stewart. He signed himself James Lawson Parker and gave his occupation and address as gamekeeper at Shieldhill, Biggar. By 1911 the Parkers had a home at Shieldhill Mains, with three children, John, Euphemia and Robert.

The Dunlops left their own marks on the parish. In 1903 Nathaniel and his daughter Ellen built Shieldhill Hall for evening worship and other gatherings. In 1911 Ellen helped establish a creamery at Thankerton, beside the railway station, where local farms could send their milk. She later helped found the Quothquan Women's Rural Institute. The hall, the creamery and the cottages all belonged to the history of the same small district.

Agnes appears in the records as a domestic servant. Her employer's name has yet to turn up. The address she gave was a cottage on the estate.

Chapter 6. Lizzie

Liberton, Edinburgh, 1874–1906

While Agnes was growing up in Lanarkshire, the woman who would raise her son was living a different life on the south side of Edinburgh. Her name was Elizabeth Miller McDonald. In the family she was Lizzie.

Lizzie was born on May 31, 1874, at Gilmerton, in the parish of Liberton, near Edinburgh. This Liberton, spelled with one b, was a different parish from the Libberton near Biggar where Agnes Brown grew up. The resemblance of those names can make two families look nearer to one another on paper than they were.

Lizzie's mother, Ann McDonald, married Robert Hope. In 1878 Ann and Robert sailed for New Zealand on the Rakaia. A son was born aboard the ship on September 26, and the New Zealand Mail reported the birth when she arrived. New Zealand's birth index names him John Rakaia Hope. According to the Hope family history, the family settled at Cross Creek in the Wairarapa. Lizzie was four when her mother emigrated. She grew up in Scotland.

In 1881 she was six and living on the Main Road at Gilmerton with her grandparents, John McDonald and Janet Page. The census calls her their grandchild and gives her occupation as scholar. Ten years later Lizzie was sixteen and working as a live-in domestic servant at 14 Wilson Road in Liberton. There were four young boys in the household and another servant, Catherine Davidson.

On June 9, 1899, at 34 St Patrick Square in Edinburgh, Lizzie married Robert Douglas. He was twenty-three, a sawmiller, the son of Archibald Douglas and Isabella Gray. The marriage entry gave Lizzie's age as twenty-four, although her twenty-fifth birthday had passed nine days earlier. In the places set aside for her parents are the names John McDonald and Janet McDonald, born Page, the grandparents in whose house the census had found her as a girl.

Family records give their daughter Janet Page Douglas a birthday of April 29, 1900. The following spring the census found the family at 5 Beaumont Place in Edinburgh. Robert was working as a stone machineman. Lizzie was twenty-six. Janet was eleven months old.

Our family also remembers a daughter called Isabella Gray Douglas who died in infancy. Her name was the name of Robert's mother. I have yet to find her birth or death entry. In 1911 the census listed ten-year-old Janet in Lizzie's household as daughter and three-year-old Laurence as son. Why she and Robert took him in was never written down in any record I have seen.

Chapter 7. The Autumn of 1906

Quothquan, Lanarkshire, 1906

In the autumn of 1906 Agnes was twenty. Five years earlier the census had listed her as the fifteen-year-old niece in her uncle's cottage. By the following June she was a domestic servant and a mother. Between those two descriptions there is very little paper.

Much of a servant's working year in the Scottish countryside turned on two term days, Whitsunday in May and Martinmas in November, when servants customarily entered a new place. A place might include board and lodging along with wages. Giving up a place could therefore mean giving up a home. None of the employment records I have found names the house in which Agnes worked that year or says what became of her place.

The summer ended in a spell of heat. At Leith the weather station registered eighty-five degrees Fahrenheit in the twenty-four hours ending on the morning of September 1. In the following weeks the same station recorded strong winds, and on September 24 a low of thirty-six degrees. Leith lay some thirty miles from Quothquan. Those observations give the season a date and a place of their own.

Counting back from a birth in the middle of June puts the likely beginning of a full-term pregnancy in the autumn. The length of Agnes's pregnancy is unknown. There is no date at which I can say whom she saw or where she was that day.

In 1901 the census placed Mary Archibald at Gean Tree, near the home Agnes shared with her uncle. Six years later, when Agnes gave her own address, she named Gean Cottage. I can trace the two women to the cottages in different years. Who shared a home with Agnes while she was expecting her son is still an open question.

Chapter 8. The Road to Edinburgh

Upper Clydesdale and Edinburgh, Spring 1907

Quothquan had no railway station. Thankerton had one, across the Clyde, about two miles away by road. A traveler could go by rail through Carstairs Junction and east toward Edinburgh. A timetable printed in May 1900 lists a morning train leaving Thankerton at 8:35 and reaching Edinburgh at 9:40, and an afternoon train at 2:35 that arrived at 3:55. The railway is one way of understanding how the village was connected to the capital. Agnes's own means of travel remain unknown.

The line toward Edinburgh passed Carnwath, where her grandmother had been born, then Auchengray and Cobbinshaw. Beyond the moor it descended toward Midlothian and the approaches to the city. Edinburgh in those years had a population of more than three hundred thousand.

The Edinburgh directory gives the hospital's address as 79 Lauriston Place. The hospital itself was a sandstone building with gabled wings and a square tower at the corner. It had opened in 1879, and a pavilion for married women had been added in 1895. Across the street was a separate maternity home. They had different doors, different arrangements and different records.

For Agnes I have two addresses. One belonged to a cottage at Shieldhill. The other is the hospital named on her son's birth entry. I can trace the distance between them on a map. I cannot say who traveled with her, when she left the cottage or how long she had been in Edinburgh before the child was born.

Chapter 9. Born at Nine O'Clock

Edinburgh, June 14, 1907

At nine o'clock on the evening of Friday, June 14, 1907, Agnes Brown gave birth to a son at the Royal Maternity Hospital in Edinburgh. She was twenty-one.

The time is one of the details the Scottish register preserves. Nine in the evening, down to the hour. On that same day, the weather station at Leith recorded calm, cloudy conditions in the morning and a temperature of fifty-nine degrees Fahrenheit at six in the evening. The next morning's report recorded drizzle and rain, with four hundredths of an inch in the twenty-four hours.

The maternity hospital was also a teaching hospital. Its physicians worked with house surgeons, students and pupil midwives. Staff attended women in the hospital and went out to deliveries in homes across Edinburgh. One of its physicians, John William Ballantyne, had been working for years to establish care for women before the birth of their babies. A bed for that purpose had been endowed there in 1901.

The institution left annual reports that describe that work. I have no ward account for Agnes that tells me who attended her or how long she stayed. The record I can put beside the family watch is the civil register. It gives the city engraved inside the case, the hospital on Lauriston Place and the hour of my grandfather's birth.

His mother was the person who supplied those particulars.

Chapter 10. His First Name

Edinburgh, July 4, 1907

On Thursday, July 4, twenty days after the birth, Agnes registered her son in the district of St Giles. Scottish law allowed twenty-one days to report a birth. The registrar was R. D. Robertson. His office was at 24 Forrest Road. The entry is number eight hundred and thirty-nine.

The boy's name was Lawrence Brown. In this first record, Lawrence is spelled with a w. In our family it was understood that she named him for her father's older brother, Lawrence Tweedie Brown. Her father George had been dead for twenty-one years.

The register gives her occupation as domestic servant and her domicile as Gean Cottage, Shieldhill, Biggar. Under the rules then in force, there were conditions for entering the name of a father who was not married to the mother. A mother could register a birth in her own name without identifying the father on the page. My grandfather's entry leaves the father's particulars blank. It tells us nothing about a conversation in the office.

The registrar signed in his own column. In the first column is the name she gave him, Lawrence Brown. Farther across the page, Agnes signed her own name. Beneath her signature is the word "Mother."

Chapter 11. The Missing Years

July 1907 – April 1911

From July 1907 to the spring of 1911, I lose him.

Agnes had given Gean Cottage as her home. That is the last address the register gives us. How long the baby stayed with Agnes and where he went afterward are still unknown.

The cottages themselves continued to turn up in the records. In 1911 Andrew Rodger and his wife were recorded at Gean Tree Cottage. James Parker and his family lived at Shieldhill Mains. Those entries help follow the people Agnes had grown up among. They do not put a little boy in any of their houses during the intervening years.

There is no date I can give my mother for the day her father came into the Douglas family. I cannot say who carried him there, how old he was, or what part Agnes had in arranging it. Almost four years of a child's life lie between two entries. The first is signed by his mother. The second gives him a place in another household.

Chapter 12. The Douglases

Edinburgh, by April 1911

On Sunday, April 2, 1911, Laurence was living at 16 Lochrin Place, in the Tollcross district of Edinburgh. After searching through the missing years, I found him within walking distance of where he had been born.

The surviving census page has Elizabeth Douglas at the top. She was thirty-six and born at Gilmerton. Janet was ten, a schoolgirl born in Edinburgh. Immediately below Janet is a boy of three, also born in Edinburgh. The surname for the family is Douglas. His given names are entered as Laurence B.

This time Laurence is spelled with a u. Read aloud, the difference disappears. On paper you can see it. Alongside his age is the word that explains how he stood in the household. Son.

The census also asked married women how many children had been born alive to their present marriage and how many were living. The figures beside Elizabeth's name are two and two. Neither figure tells us which children she counted. A tailor from Caithness, a boarder aged twenty-five, also appears on the page.

Robert, Lizzie and Janet are the people who recur in the boy's own life. Robert would be his guardian on the school register. Janet would be his sister on the journey to America. When a ship's list asked him for the nearest relative he was leaving behind in Scotland, he gave Lizzie's name and called her his mother.

Chapter 13. Agnes Marries

Morningside, Edinburgh, 1910–1919

Five months after the census found her son at Lochrin Place, Agnes appears under her full name in another Edinburgh register. By the summer of 1911 she was an asylum nurse at West House, part of the Royal Edinburgh Asylum in Morningside. She was twenty-five. A young man who gave the same address worked there as an attendant. His name was Thomas McKay McKenzie.

On August 28, 1911, the two married by declaration at 34 Chambers Street. Their witnesses were an asylum attendant and a coachman. A warrant from the sheriff-substitute was dated that day. The registrar's signature is familiar. R. D. Robertson had also signed the birth entry of Lawrence Brown.

Thomas was twenty-one and the son of a coastguardsman. Agnes gave the names of George Brown and Christina Henderson as her parents. Both were dead. George's occupation appears as farm grieve, a man who supervised work on a farm. The earlier records from Edston call him a plowman. The bride signed her name as Agnes McGarth Brown.

At ten in the morning on May 6, 1912, Agnes gave birth to Mary Christina McKenzie at 9 Stewart Terrace, Edinburgh. In August 1914 she had another daughter, Jean Henderson McKenzie. The family names from Clydesdale continued in both girls.

Thomas joined the army in December 1915. He was working as a barman by then. He named Agnes and the two girls on his family papers and gave an Edinburgh address on Balcarres Street. Across the Forth, Agnes's older child was eight years old and enrolled at school under the name Douglas.

Chapter 14. Lawrence Douglas

Dunfermline, Fife, 1912

On August 19, 1912, the Queen Anne Infant School in Dunfermline admitted a boy named Lawrence Douglas. The register gives his date of birth as June 14, 1907. He was five. It was his first school.

The family now lived across the Forth from Edinburgh, at 36 Victoria Terrace. Robert Douglas appears in the space for the boy's parent or guardian. Beside it, in the column asking how the child was related to him, someone wrote a single word. Adopted.

It is more than I had learned from the census. There, at the age of three, he had simply been Lizzie's son. Here was someone putting on paper that he had come into the family by adoption. The Scottish law that provided for adoption by court order was still eighteen years away. The school was recording the relationship as it was understood in their home.

Two days later, the primary department entered Janet Douglas in its own register. She was twelve. She had come from the Commercial School. Her address was also 36 Victoria Terrace, and her guardian was Robert.

Seven years separated the children. For Janet there was an earlier school to put in the proper column. For Lawrence there was none. They had come to the same school, from the same home, and they would still be giving each other's names to officials on the other side of the Atlantic seventeen years later.

Chapter 15. A Boy in Dunfermline

Dunfermline, Fife, 1912–1916

Dunfermline was a town of linen mills on the high ground north of the Forth. Its abbey held the tomb of Robert the Bruce. At the foot of the hill was the glen at Pittencrieff, which Andrew Carnegie, a weaver's son born in Dunfermline, had bought for the people of his hometown.

Carnegie had also given the town a public library. It opened in 1883, the first of the libraries he funded. The words over the door were "Let There Be Light." My family remembers that my grandfather loved to read. His school register gives me his address in Dunfermline. I would like to know what books he read there when he was a boy.

On August 1, 1914, Janet left school at fourteen. Laurence was seven when Britain entered the war three days later. Down by the water, the Admiralty had been building a naval dockyard at Rosyth. By December, British battlecruisers were based in the Forth.

On January 11, 1916, Laurence moved up to the primary department at Queen Anne. Robert was still his guardian. This time the home address was 5 Foundry Street. He was eight years old.

That May the battlecruisers sailed from the Forth to fight off Jutland. The Queen Mary and the Indefatigable were among the ships lost. Others returned to the Forth on June 2. By then civilians on the south shore had already experienced a night of the war in their own streets. One of those streets was Lauriston Place, where Laurence had been born.

Chapter 16. Fire Over Edinburgh

Edinburgh and Leith, April 2–3, 1916

Late on Sunday, April 2, 1916, a German airship came up the Forth. Around half past eleven the first bombs fell on Leith. A whisky warehouse caught fire. The account the chief constable of Leith gave afterward follows the damage street by street. Warnings had already gone out, lights had been lowered and traffic stopped.

In Edinburgh, a bomb struck the house of Doctor McLaren at 39 Lauriston Place. Part of the roof and a wall came away. The McLaren family escaped injury. In nearby Graham Street, David Robertson was struck by a fragment and later died at the Royal Infirmary. At 79 Lauriston Place stood the Royal Maternity Hospital.

After midnight another bomb fell outside an apartment building on Marshall Street. Among the dead were John Smith and his fifteen-year-old son William. David Graham was five. One can follow the route of that airship on a map, but these names tell something the route alone cannot. In one Edinburgh household that night, a father and his boy both died.

Thomas had given the army an Edinburgh address for Agnes in December. Across the water in Dunfermline, Laurence's January school entry gives the address on Foundry Street. I don't know what either of them saw that night. I do know that the house hit on Lauriston Place stood on the very street where Agnes had given birth to him.

Chapter 17. The House on Foundry Street

Dunfermline, Fife, 1919–1926

On September 9, 1919, Elizabeth Douglas reported the death of her husband. Robert had died at half past seven that morning at Woodburn Sanatorium in Morningside, on the Edinburgh side of the Forth. He was forty-three. He had been ill with tuberculosis of the lungs for about a year. His trade was entered as stone machineman. Lizzie gave her address as 5 Foundry Street, Dunfermline, and signed as his widow.

Laurence was twelve years old. A year later, on October 8, 1920, the education authority granted him an exemption that allowed him to leave school. The entry gives a date. It says nothing about his first day's work or what arrangements his mother made. By the following summer, all three members of the family had jobs.

The census was taken on June 19, 1921, five days after Laurence turned fourteen. Lizzie, forty-seven, was cleaning schools for the Fife Education Authority. Janet, twenty-one, was a clerk with the Dunfermline Co-operative Society. Laurence was a message boy for McClelland and Company, a grocer. A saleswoman named Margaret Dobson boarded with them. The four of them occupied three rooms.

For children under fifteen the census asked whether either parent had died. Beside Laurence's name the answer was abbreviated to two letters, meaning father dead. On the 1907 birth entry the space for a father had been empty. At fourteen he was counted as a boy who had lost his father. I know the name of the man his school had entered as his guardian, the man whose death Lizzie registered. He was Robert Douglas.

Chapter 18. Janet Sails First

Dunfermline, July–September 1926

Janet was the first of the two children from Foundry Street to leave Scotland. On July 16, 1926, she married Thomas Crombie in Dunfermline. He came from Kinross. She was twenty-six and he was thirty-two. Years later, when the two of them applied for American citizenship, each gave the same date and place for their marriage.

That summer there was a miners' lockout in Britain. Fife was coal country, and the naval dockyard at Rosyth had been reduced to a maintenance base. Those were changes anyone looking for work in the district had to reckon with. Janet left no account that tells me why she and Thomas chose Chicago.

On September 13, less than two months after the wedding, Janet entered the United States at Detroit by rail. Chicago would be her home. There Thomas worked as a furniture salesman. When members of the family came from Scotland, the American address they gave again and again was the Crombies' on South Kostner Avenue.

Her brother was nineteen when Janet left. Their school entries had put them together as children. The American records call her Mrs. Crombie, an easy name to miss if you are looking for a girl named Douglas. Once I knew to look for it, I began finding my grandfather beside his sister again.

Chapter 19. Last Days in Scotland

Dunfermline, spring–September 1929

On May 18, 1929, the Montroyal landed at Greenock from Quebec. Among the third-class passengers was Janet Crombie. Her permanent home was in the United States. For her address in Britain she gave 39 Grieve Street, Dunfermline. In September, both Janet and Laurence would give that same Grieve Street address for their mother, Mrs. Elizabeth Anderson.

There is a photograph in the family of Janet and Laurence eating ice cream. She has a cone in each hand. He is much taller than she is, leaning against a railing with one knee bent. His jacket is open over a sweater and tie. There is a white handkerchief in his breast pocket. I do not have a date or a place for the picture. I like it because in the official records these two are usually schoolchildren, workers, passengers or relatives at an address. Here they are a sister and a brother eating ice cream.

On September 3, the American consulate at Glasgow issued his immigration visa. He had been Douglas at school and Douglas at the grocer's on the census. On his travel papers he is Brown again, the surname Agnes had given him at birth. The American papers spell his first name Lawrence, with a w. The British list spells it Laurence.

I don't know when Laurence learned about Agnes, or what he knew of her when he sailed. What I can say is that he was twenty-two, his mother in Dunfermline was the woman he called Elizabeth Anderson, and he was booked to sail on the same ship as his sister.

Chapter 20. The Crossing

Greenock to Detroit, September 7–15, 1929

On Saturday, September 7, 1929, the Duchess of Bedford sailed from Greenock. Janet Crombie and Lawrence Brown were listed in cabin class. The ship belonged to Canadian Pacific and had been built at Clydebank by John Brown and Company. My grandfather went out of Scotland on a ship built on the river from which he sailed.

His border card gives September 13 as the day he reached Quebec. Two days later, at Detroit, Laurence was examined for admission to the United States. The border papers give a description of him that a daughter or a grandchild can still recognize. Six feet tall. Fair hair. Blue eyes. Occupation, grocer.

He had paid his own passage. He had fifty dollars and meant to stay. He gave his reason for coming as seeking work. For his nearest relative in Scotland, he named his mother, Mrs. Elizabeth Anderson, at 39 Grieve Street. For the person he was going to in America, he named his brother-in-law, Thomas Crombie, at 1534 South Kostner Avenue in Chicago.

One form, filled out for an American inspector, holds quite a bit of our family. The passenger's own surname was Brown. The mother he left behind was Anderson. The man he was going to was Crombie. The relationship between them all runs through the Douglas household in which he and Janet grew up.

We have always understood that the pocket watch my grandmother gave me came across with him. That part of the crossing comes to me from home. What survives in his own hand from Detroit is the signature of a young man entering America as Lawrence Brown.

Chapter 21. Chicago

Chicago, Ill., 1929–1936

The first American census in which my grandfather appears is the one taken in the spring of 1930. To find him you have to turn the page. Near the bottom of one sheet on South Kostner Avenue in Chicago are Thomas and Janet Crombie. At the top of the next sheet is Lawrence Brown, twenty-two, Thomas's brother-in-law. Thomas sold furniture. Laurence was a salesman in a grocery.

South Kostner ran through North Lawndale on the city's West Side. The census gives the Crombies' number as 1532. On his way into the country the previous September, Laurence had given it as 1534. The name Brown alone would make him hard to pick out in an Illinois grocery. Janet's name and the word brother-in-law make the connection. Chicago records from that same year also give his name as Lawrence B. Douglas. He was using both surnames in America by 1930.

The stock market had broken six weeks after he entered the country. Chicago suffered badly in the Depression that followed. Even the public schools fell behind in paying their teachers. The grocery job is one piece of my grandfather's working life that I can date to that first spring. I have no run of pay slips or letters to tell me how he managed through the lean years.

In October 1934 another member of the Foundry Street household arrived at the American border. Elizabeth Anderson gave her daughter Janet as the person she was joining. Her destination was 1518 South Kostner Avenue, just down the street from the address her son had given five years before. Lizzie's own card puts her journey in the family story. Whether and when she and Laurence saw each other again is something I would still like to know.

Laurence later gave Chicago as his home in the spring of 1935. In October 1936 Janet had twins, Elizabeth, called Betty, and Jessie. The next time I can place their uncle at work, it is halfway across the country, in a trade he would follow for the rest of his life.

Chapter 22. Weybosset Street

Providence, R.I., 1937–1938

In the Providence directory for 1937 there is a short entry for Lawrence Douglas. His business address was 165 Weybosset Street. He lived at 35 Elmwood Avenue. His occupation was display manager.

The address on Weybosset belonged to the John Mason Building, a six-story building across the street from the Outlet department store. Westminster Street ran nearby. In a district where people passed from shop to shop on foot, the part of a clothing store a person could see from the sidewalk was a workplace in its own right. Dressing those windows was the work my grandfather had found.

His family remembered his own appearance. His hair was carefully combed and his clothes were well kept. In the photograph with Janet you can even see the handkerchief folded into his jacket pocket. The picture and the recollection tell me something about the man who dressed the windows. They give me no way of knowing who taught him the trade or what his first window looked like.

The following year the directory still lists him at 165 Weybosset. By then his home address was 459 Pine Street. Across two years, with two places to live, the work address held steady. In the 1937 directory, another entry gives a woman's name and a place of work just one street away. She was a hairdresser at 290 Westminster. Her name was Constance Darcy.

Chapter 23. Constance

Providence, R.I., 1888–1938

Before she was Mrs. Douglas, my grandmother was a Providence girl named Constance Eugenie Darcy. She was born on October 12, 1912, at 410 Smith Street. When she came into the world my grandfather was five years old and had just begun school in Dunfermline.

Her father was Albert Blake Darcy. Her mother, Eugenie Emma Delaire, had been born at Sutton in Quebec. They married in Providence on January 16, 1912. Albert was twenty-three and Eugenie seventeen. The year after their daughter's birth, Eugenie's widowed mother, Julia Delaire, also appears at 410 Smith Street. For a little while the Providence directory gives us three generations at one address.

Constance's younger brother, Albert Norman Darcy, was born in Taunton, Massachusetts, in December 1915. Their father worked as a linotype operator. It was a newspaper trade in which the operator sat at a keyboard and the machine cast whole lines of type in metal. Albert worked for the Pawtucket Times and later for the Providence Journal. One of those papers would carry the notice of his son-in-law's death.

In 1930 Constance was seventeen, at school and living with her mother at 111 Pratt Street in Providence. The census gives Eugenie's occupation as interior decorator in a rug works. Their rent was twenty-eight dollars a month. By 1937 Constance had a trade herself, hairdressing at 290 Westminster Street, room 506. The following year she was in room 402 of the same building, where the directory lists a beauty shop.

In our family people remember how easy she was to be with. I don't have the story of the first time they met. I have their two work addresses, and I have what came of it.

Chapter 24. The Hurricane of 1938

Providence, R.I., September 21, 1938

On Wednesday, September 21, 1938, a hurricane drove water up Narragansett Bay and into downtown Providence.

It had rained in New England for four days, and the rivers were already high. Far to the south, a hurricane was moving up the Atlantic coast. The Weather Bureau had tracked it for days, and forecasters expected it to curve out to sea, as such storms usually did. Instead it raced north at fifty miles an hour. Newspapers later called it the Long Island Express. By the time the bureau warned that it would cross Long Island and Connecticut, it was already close to shore. It crossed Long Island that afternoon and came ashore in Connecticut near high tide.

Downtown Providence stood at the narrow end of Narragansett Bay, at the tip of a funnel nearly thirty miles long. Whatever water the wind drove up the bay had nowhere to go but into the city.

On Weybosset Street, across from the Outlet department store, was the building where Laurence Douglas dressed windows. Where he and Constance were that afternoon, no record I have found says. To see what the day was like, there are people who lived through it and told their own stories afterward.

One of them was Loraine Martin, who was to marry Josef Fogel that evening at the Narragansett Hotel on Dorrance Street. Miss Dyer, the society editor of The Providence Journal, telephoned to ask if the wedding would go ahead. Loraine told her it would. The PBS documentary The Hurricane of '38 says the bridegroom spent hours stranded at Union Station.

By mid-afternoon the wind was tearing through Rhode Island. In East Providence, a chimney crashed through the slate roof of the town hall. In Woonsocket, a falling sign missed a man by about a foot. In downtown Providence, Doris Layden was riding in her father's car toward the cathedral when she saw the whole front window of a furniture store come out in one piece. The wind carried it up the street, shattering as it went. She could hear other windows going all around her. Outside Boston, at the Blue Hill Observatory, instruments recorded a gust of one hundred eighty-six miles an hour.

Along the shore in Westerly, the storm surge swept away summer cottages at Watch Hill and Misquamicut. At Narragansett Pier it swept away hotels and beach pavilions. The water came up the bay past Newport and Prudence Island. On Prudence, the lighthouse keeper's house was swept away, and his wife and son drowned. The water continued past Warwick and into the Providence River.

It reached downtown at about five o'clock, as office workers and shoppers were starting home. In Exchange Place, in front of City Hall, the water rose from a few inches deep to waist high in minutes. It ran up Weybosset Street and through the doors of the shops. Loraine saw parked cars swept along Dorrance Street toward the river. As the water reached the wiring of the flooded cars, their horns began to sound. Years afterward, Doris Layden still remembered hearing those horns through the water. The wind gauge at the Weather Bureau recorded eighty-seven miles an hour before it blew away. At the Biltmore Hotel, people threw bedsheets from the second floor for those caught in the current to grab. A steamer, the Monhegan, was driven aground and tipped over in the harbor channel at the edge of downtown.

When the water crested at Market Square, it stood thirteen feet eight and a half inches above mean high water. A bronze plaque on the Market House records the height. It was nearly two feet higher than the mark left by the great gale of 1815, the worst flood the city had known until then.

By nightfall the water was draining back toward the bay. Downtown was dark. The National Guard began patrolling the streets while the water was still receding. Across southern New England, the storm killed 564 people and injured more than seventeen hundred. Nearly nine thousand homes and buildings were destroyed, and some twenty-six hundred boats were lost. In Rhode Island, estimates of the dead run as high as four hundred, most of them in towns along the shore.

The Fogels were married by candlelight that evening. Years later, Josef remembered the ceremony as early. Loraine said it was late. She also remembered the water pouring into shops on Weybosset Street, where my grandfather worked. Where he and Constance were when the water came up is something I never learned.

It was not the last time the bay came into the city in their lifetime. In August 1954, Hurricane Carol followed almost the same path and put downtown Providence under water again. By then Laurence and Constance were living in Providence with their three girls. After that second flood, the city and the Army Corps of Engineers decided to wall off the harbor. Work on the Fox Point Hurricane Barrier began in 1960, the year before Laurence died. It was finished in 1966, and the bay has not flooded downtown Providence since.

Chapter 25. A Wedding in Alabama

Birmingham, Ala., October 9, 1939

On February 10, 1939, a daughter was born in Attleboro, Massachusetts. Her name was Eugenie Elizabeth. When asked to list his children a few years later, Laurence gave her name, her birthplace and the date himself. In the next census she appears as a little girl born in Massachusetts, living with Laurence and Constance.

She had a grandmother named Eugenie on her mother's side of the family and a grandmother named Elizabeth in the Douglas family. Both names had already made a long journey to reach this child, one by way of Quebec and the other from Scotland.

On Monday, October 9, 1939, Laurence and Constance married in Birmingham, Alabama. Their marriage papers give the bride's name as Constance Darcy and the groom's as Lawrence Douglas. The address he gave was on South Hamlin Avenue in Chicago. Hers was 454 Broadway in Providence. Their marriage was performed by Thomas R. Lea, a judge of the municipal court.

It is an unexpected place to come upon two people our family knew in Rhode Island. Their papers preserve the name of the judge and the addresses Laurence gave for the two of them. There is no family account of how they came to choose Birmingham or who saw them married. The next day two Birmingham newspapers carried their names.

Six months later Laurence, Constance and little Eugenie were together at a new address. This one was a rented home in San Francisco.

Chapter 26. San Francisco

San Francisco, Calif., 1940–1941

On April 11, 1940, a census taker came to Valencia Street in San Francisco. The Douglas family paid thirty-three dollars a month in rent. Laurence worked in a clothing store. The words used for his trade were window trimmer. He had worked forty-eight hours in the week the census asked about. For the whole of the previous year he reported earnings of two thousand two hundred and forty dollars.

The census asked where each person had lived five years earlier. For Laurence the answer was Chicago. For Constance it was Providence. Their daughter was one year old. The three birthplaces in the household were Scotland, Rhode Island and Massachusetts. Now the three of them lived in California.

In October, when Laurence registered for the draft, he gave a new address, 129 Julian Avenue. His employer was Bond Stores, at Post and Kearny streets, near Union Square. For the person who would always know where to reach him, he gave his wife, Constance.

It was in San Francisco that my mother was born. Her parents called her Constance Anderson Douglas. She had her mother's first name. Anderson was the name Laurence had used for Lizzie at the Canadian border. My mother's birthday is February 2. California's birth index gives the year as 1941.

In June 1941 Laurence and his wife returned from Canada by motor coach at Blaine, Washington. The inspector put down his occupation as displayman and their San Francisco address on Mirabel Avenue. It was nearly twelve years since Laurence had first entered the country at Detroit with fifty dollars and the name of a brother-in-law in Chicago. Now he gave an address on the Pacific coast, and Constance was beside him.

Chapter 27. The Night of the Sirens

San Francisco, Calif., December 1941

My family remembers a time in the autumn of 1941 when Laurence was in Pittsburgh and Constance was still in San Francisco. Two men in Pennsylvania later said they had known him since that September. Laurence would later ask both men to speak for him when he applied for citizenship.

On Sunday, December 7, the news of the attack on Pearl Harbor reached San Francisco late in the morning. Volunteers began reporting for duty that afternoon. By the following evening the authorities were ordering a blackout. Just after six on Monday, people who had been listening all day for news heard the alarms in their own city.

Police cars and fire engines sounded warnings. Some electric signs had been set on timers and stayed lit while people tried to darken the streets. The San Francisco Chronicle reported four alarms that night. No bombs fell on San Francisco. The people who lived through those hours only learned that as the night passed.

I cannot place my grandmother in a particular house that evening or say what news got through to my grandfather. They had lived on the Pacific coast together that summer. The next address at which all four of them can be placed is in Pennsylvania. In his papers there, Laurence gave the names of his wife and the two little girls who lived with him.

Chapter 28. An American Citizen

Pittsburgh and Avalon, Pa., 1941–1943

Laurence Brown Douglas was living at 612 California Avenue in Avalon, just outside Pittsburgh, when he petitioned for American citizenship. He was a display manager for Bond Stores. The papers describe him as five feet eleven, with blue eyes and brown hair. They give his date of birth as June 14, 1907. More than thirty-five years after Agnes registered him in Edinburgh, her son was giving the same date for himself in Pennsylvania.

Two men in the Pittsburgh area vouched for him on February 11, 1943. David Hunter was a machinist. Frank Holliday was a clerk. On March 3, two people who had known him in California gave depositions in San Francisco. Their names were William Howard Vahlkamp and Betty Johnstone Sherwood. His move across the country meant that some of the people able to speak for him lived more than two thousand miles away.

He named his wife, Constance Darcy, and gave Birmingham as the place of their marriage. He listed Eugenie Elizabeth and Constance Anderson as his daughters. He also gave the date on which he had entered the United States at Detroit and the name he had used on entry. Lawrence Brown.

On May 6, 1943, he took the oath and was admitted as a citizen of the United States. On the record of that day his name is Laurence Brown Douglas.

It is one of the reasons I can tell my mother with confidence that the boy registered in Edinburgh, the young grocer who came through Detroit, and the man who raised her were the same person. On the citizenship papers he brought the names together himself.

Chapter 29. River Avenue

Providence and Pawtucket, R.I., 1945–1960

By 1947 the family was back in Rhode Island. The Pawtucket directory gives their address as 97 Magill Street. Laurence was dressing windows. In Providence by 1950, he and Constance were living at 148 General Street. They remained in the directories there for several years. In 1956 the address changed to 644 River Avenue.

Eugenie Elizabeth, Constance Anderson and Norma were the three Douglas girls. Their father's work took him into stores around Pawtucket. It is easier now to find an address where he worked than a photograph of a window he dressed. A store could change its windows every season. The family kept a clearer picture of the man. He was quiet. He loved to read. They remembered how particular he was about his appearance, right down to his hair.

While the girls were growing up, their mother's family was nearby. In October 1954 her brother, Albert Norman Darcy, died at thirty-eight. Their father, Albert Blake Darcy, the newspaper linotype operator, died in 1957. Janet and Thomas were still in Illinois. Both had become American citizens too, Thomas in 1947 and Janet the following spring.

The directory for 1960 names one of the shops where Laurence worked, Carter's Men Store in Pawtucket. The next directory gives his business in two words, window displays, and prints 644 River Avenue as the address. By the end of his life he was working for himself.

Beside him in the directory is Constance, a nurse at Roger Williams General Hospital. She had trained at the Rhode Island School of Practical Nursing. I first came across the two of them in separate entries in a Providence directory, a hairdresser on Westminster and a display manager on Weybosset. Almost a quarter of a century later, the same kind of book puts their two occupations under the same roof.

Chapter 30. The Ninth of March

Providence, R.I., March 1961

It came out of nowhere, the way the worst things in his life always had.

In the winter of 1961 he was fifty-three years old, a tall, quiet man in a pressed suit who went out every morning with his tools and his pins and his good eye to dress other people's windows. He was not an old man. He had a business of his own now, a wife who loved him, a family, and a house on River Avenue with snow on the porch rail. There was every reason to think there were years ahead.

Then he was ill. And then, suddenly, he was in the hospital.

Nobody at home had known. That is what the family remembered afterward, more than anything: how little warning there was. One week he was going to work. The next he was lying in a bed at St. Joseph's Hospital in South Providence, and the doctors were saying words that no one wanted to hear. The family understood it to be lung cancer, and that it had been growing in him, silent, for longer than anyone could have guessed. By the time anyone knew, there was nothing anyone could do.

Picture Constance in those days. She was a nurse. She knew what the doctors' faces meant before they said a word. She had sat beside other people's husbands and fathers in beds like his and held other women's hands. Now it was her own husband, and all her training, all her gentleness, all the gift she had for putting frightened people at ease, came down to sitting beside him, holding his hand, and trying not to let him see how afraid she was.

Thursday, March 9, 1961. A storm came up the coast in the dark, and all that day Providence had fog, and snow, and sleet, and rain, and the streets and trees and wires began to glaze over with ice. The city went slow and quiet and gray.

That day, at St. Joseph's Hospital, Laurence Brown Douglas died.

He was fifty-three years, eight months and twenty-three days old.

He had been born in a maternity hospital in Edinburgh to a young woman with no one beside her. He had been given into the arms of a woman who had once been left behind herself. He had walked to school with a forester's son, grown up beside a war that set his birthplace on fire, lost the father who raised him at twelve, carried groceries up the steep streets of Dunfermline, and stood at the rail of a ship and watched Scotland disappear. He had come through the Depression on a grocer's wage and become an artist of windows. He had survived the sea in the streets of Providence, married the girl a block away, crossed America twice, sworn himself a citizen in the middle of a war, and raised a family in a house that was his own.

And now, on a gray, icy day in March, in a hospital bed in Providence, it was over.

On Saturday, the Pawtucket Times printed his obituary on page three. It said he had been born in Edinburgh, Scotland. It said he was the son of the late Robert and Elizabeth (Anderson) Douglas. It said he had worked for the Bond Clothing Company, and had his own display business, and that for the past fifteen years he had dressed windows for Pawtucket firms. It named his wife, Constance, his family, and his sister, Mrs. Thomas Crombie of Chicago.

It did not name Agnes Brown.

The newspaper gave the facts of a working life. The family remembered other things. His friends called him Doug. He was always impeccably dressed, his hair perfectly combed. He was quiet. He loved to read.

On Monday, March 13, they buried him at Saint Ann Cemetery in Cranston, in the family lot where Constance's young brother, Albert, already lay.

Later they raised a stone.

LAURENCE B. DOUGLAS
1907–1961

Look closely at it. Between his first name and the name of the family that raised him, the stone keeps a single letter: B.

B, for Brown.

The name his mother gave him in an office on Forrest Road in 1907, on the morning she signed the register alone. The name that stayed with him, as an initial, when the Douglases took him in. The name that went with him to school, across the ocean and into the courtroom in Pittsburgh. Fifty-three years later, after everything, it was still with him, cut into granite in a cemetery in Rhode Island, a single letter holding a whole lost life.

Thirty-three years later, the same stone would carry Constance's name too.

Epilogue. The Names He Carried

Rhode Island, 1961–1994

He was gone. But the women who had carried him were not finished with his story.

Constance was forty-eight when her husband died, a widow with a house full of his things. She did what she had always done. She kept going. She kept nursing, at the Briarcliffe Health Center in Johnston and then for eight years at Cranston General Hospital, sitting beside the sick and the frightened and putting them at ease, until she retired in 1974. Her mother, Eugenie, died in 1966 and was buried at Saint Ann, not far from Laurence.

In her last years Constance lived in East Greenwich, on Sixth Street, and went to Mass at St. Francis de Sales. She was the same as she had always been: pleasant, warm, easy to be with, the kind of woman people were glad to sit beside. She died at Rhode Island Hospital on Tuesday, April 12, 1994, at eighty-one. They buried her beside him, in lot 84 at Saint Ann, thirty-three years after him, and cut her name into the stone next to his.

Janet, the big sister who had walked him up the hill to school and taken him across the ocean, lived out her life in Chicago with Thomas and their twin girls. She died in December 1979, at seventy-nine.

Of Lizzie, the woman who had been left behind at four and grew up to take him in, the records keep only a little more: a new name, Anderson, and a house on Grieve Street in Dunfermline, the address he gave for his mother the day he sailed. By the time he died, his obituary called her "the late Elizabeth." She had let him go so that he could have a life. He had. Every time he signed his name, Douglas, he carried hers.

And Agnes.

Agnes McGarth Brown, who let him go so that he could have more than she could give, married Thomas McKenzie in 1911 and raised two daughters, Mary and Jean. One portrait of her is known: a head-and-shoulders picture of a woman with a steady, serious face. Whether she ever learned what became of the boy she gave away, whether she ever knew that he crossed the ocean, made a life and married, no record says. But she had given him the only things she had to give: his life, and his name. And through everything, through Dunfermline and Chicago and San Francisco and the courtroom in Pittsburgh, he kept the name. It is on his stone.

He carried more than one name, and each one was a gift from someone who loved him.

He was born Lawrence Brown, the son of Agnes, who carried him through a frightened winter and could not keep him.

He grew up Laurence Douglas, the son of Robert and Lizzie, who took in another woman's child and loved him as their own.

He crossed the ocean as Lawrence Brown again, because to begin his new life he had to go back to the first one.

And in 1943, in a federal court in Pittsburgh, he became a citizen of the United States as Laurence Brown Douglas: both names at once, the mother who bore him and the family that raised him, side by side.

To his friends, he was Doug.

I set out to give my grandfather a voice. But more than that, I did it for his daughter—my mother, Constance Douglas, who is eighty-five now. She remembered her father too, and when she spoke of him there was always a little more sadness in it than I ever heard from Nan. I wanted to give him back to her: to make his life real and tangible again.

And somewhere along the way, going back through all of it, I learned that there is nothing to be sad about. Laurence Brown Douglas led an amazing life. From the very first he was surrounded by people who loved him and who guided him—none more than his older sister, Janet, who went ahead of him across the ocean and was waiting on the other side. For all the empty spaces on his papers, he was never as alone as they made him look. He was carried, the whole way.

His watch is on my desk as I write this. It is small and heavy and worn smooth at the edges, the way metal gets when it has lived for years in a man's pocket and been taken out a thousand times to check the hour. The family says he carried it across the Atlantic in 1929, and kept it all his life.

I open the case. Inside there is one word, the name of the city where it all began:

Edinburgh.
