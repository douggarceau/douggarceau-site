The Names He Carried

The Laurence Brown Douglas Story

Prologue. The Watch

Providence, R.I., March 9, 1961

I am sitting here with an antique pocket watch in my hand. It was my grandfather's.

He died in 1961, five years before I was born, and the watch stayed with my grandmother, whom everyone called Nan. One day she set it in my hand and whispered, “This belonged to your grandfather. He brought it over from Scotland. You have it now.” Inside the case, engraved in the metal, was a single word: Edinburgh.

When I was a little boy, Nan would take me to McDonald’s in her dark brown 1960s Valiant, and our talk always found its way back to Laurence. She would smile and tell me how much I reminded her of him, and that I was named after him. She spoke of him with warmth and a quiet sadness, and she never remarried. But she never told me where in Scotland he came from, or why he left.

So I went looking for him, through ships’ manifests and city directories, until I could bring him back to life on the page.

When he died, at fifty-three, his obituary said he was born in Edinburgh to Robert and Elizabeth Douglas. It did not say that his birth was recorded under another name, to another mother. On the day he was born, he was Lawrence Brown.

This is the story of how he came by the names he carried.

Part One: Clydesdale

Chapter 1. The Plowmen

Peeblesshire, Scotland, 1835–1871

On census night in 1911 he was three years old, and he was the son of the house.

He lived with Lizzie Douglas and her daughter Janet in an apartment at Tollcross, in Edinburgh, and Robert Douglas was the man who would raise him. A year later the family crossed the Forth to Dunfermline, and he went with them, to a new flat, a new school and the big library up the hill. He was an ordinary, happy boy, and he followed the man and woman who were raising him the way any small boy follows his father and mother. They were the only parents he would ever remember, and fifty years later, when he died in Rhode Island, his obituary named them as his mother and father.

But there was another family behind him, with other names, and a watch with one word inside it. To find them you have to go back to the hills of the Scottish Borders, half a century before he was born, to a family of plowmen.

The river Tweed rises high in the hills of Peeblesshire, near the great green hollow called the Devil's Beef Tub, and runs east past Tweedsmuir, Stobo and Lyne toward the market town of Peebles. It is high, bare sheep country, and the people who worked it were as hardy as the land.

Agnes McGarth was born at Tweedsmuir around 1840. The McGarths were hill people. In 1851 the census found her father, Tweedie McGarth, and her mother, Jane, a woman from Wamphray in Dumfriesshire, with a house full of children: Jane, Agnes, James Tweedie, Sarah, Ann and Janet. Their names would outlast them. "Tweedie" and "McGarth" were handed down as middle names for three generations, a way of remembering who you came from, and the proudest thing a family could leave.

Agnes married William Brown, a plowman. He walked behind a pair of horses in all weathers, turning the stony ground for oats and turnips, and he was good at it. A plowman's wages came partly in money and partly in oatmeal, potatoes, milk and coal, and his cottage came with the job. When the year's work ended at the term, a plowman's family loaded its furniture onto a farm cart, set the children up on top, and rolled on to the next farm.

Who the plowmen were

In the Borders the skilled plowman was called a hind. He was a family man, hired for a whole year at a time, usually in March or at Whitsunday, and paid partly in cash and partly in kind, with a cottage and a garden for his household.

Hiring was done face to face at the fairs. Men stood in the market and the farmers came round to judge their strength and skill and strike a bargain. At the Carnwath fair, a few miles from Quothquan, a good plowman in 1825 earned six or seven pounds for the half year, with his house, meal and potatoes, his coal carted at no charge, food at harvest and sometimes milk. By 1862 the best men earned twelve pounds or more for the half year.

At the end of the term a family might load its furniture on a cart and move to the next farm. It was hard, honest work, and it fed a household.

William and Agnes raised a big family, ten children or more. Two of their boys matter to this story.

Lawrence Tweedie Brown was born on September 14, 1860, in Peebles. George was born on July 18, 1862. In 1871 the census found them together under their father's roof, Lawrence ten and George eight, two boys who had grown up walking the furrows behind their father.

Lawrence would marry Isabella Graham from Cambusnethan, raise a family at Traquair and Sanquhar, and settle at last in Quebec. He died in 1943. Forty-seven years after his own birth, his niece would give his name to her son.

George stayed with the land. He followed his father behind the plow, and at twenty he married Christina Henderson at Quothquan, in the upper valley of the Clyde. They had two little daughters. But George did not live to see them grown.

One of those daughters was Agnes. She would give her son her uncle's name, Lawrence, and before he was four years old he would carry another.

Chapter 2. Agnes

Edston, Peebles, 1883–1886

She was born in a plowman's cottage a week before Christmas, in the dark of a Border winter, and before the snow was off the hills she had lost her father.

Her story starts two and a half years earlier, on a summer day in the upper valley of the Clyde.

On Friday, June 1, 1883, at Quothquan, in the parish of Libberton, George Brown married Christina Henderson. He was twenty, a young plowman with strong hands and his whole life in front of him. She was twenty-three, the eldest daughter of Alexander Henderson and Jane Robb, a Libberton family through and through. Her people lived under Quothquan Law, a bald round hill above the river, four miles from the market town of Biggar. Across the Clyde, filling the southwest sky, stood Tinto, the red hill of Clydesdale, with a great cairn on its summit that the old folk said had been carried up stone by stone. Christina had grown up with that hill in her window. So would her daughters.

That August their first child was born at Quothquan, a girl, and they named her Mary Henderson Brown. Christina's little brother Andrew, born when she was already sixteen, was hardly more than a boy himself. Years later he would matter a great deal to this family.

A plowman went where the work was, and by the winter of 1885 George had found a place across the county line, at Edston, a farm in the parish of Lyne, a few miles west of Peebles. It was a fresh start. The farm cottages there, low and stone-built under slate roofs, still stand along the road. The young family moved in: George, Christina and little Mary, not yet two and a half. Cottages like theirs had a coal fire in the grate and a box bed set into the wall, with the horses in the stable across the yard and the hills all around. They had steady work, a roof, each other, and a second child on the way.

At half past two in the afternoon on Friday, December 18, 1885, Christina gave birth to another girl.

On the last day of the year George went into Peebles to register her, and he signed the book himself. He named her for his own mother: Agnes McGarth Brown. It was his gift to her, the family name from the hills of Tweedsmuir.

The new year came in. The days began to lengthen. Out in the fields the ground was hardening toward the spring plowing, the busiest weeks of a plowman's year, and George was a strong young man of twenty-three who worked outdoors every day of the year.

Then, in March, with no warning, an illness took hold in his chest. It was pleuro-pneumonia, an infection of the lungs and the lining around them, and in 1886 no doctor anywhere had a medicine that could stop it. There would be no antibiotics for another half century. For three weeks he fought it in the cottage at Edston, with Christina and their two little daughters around him.

George Brown died at half past one in the morning on Monday, April 5, 1886. The register gives the causes as pleuro-pneumonia, of twenty-one days, and exhaustion. He was twenty-three years old. His baby daughter was three and a half months old. It was his own father, William Brown, the plowman, who went to the registrar to report his son's death.

Christina was a widow at twenty-six. The cottage at Edston went with the job, and the job had died with George. So she gathered up what they had and took her girls home to Libberton and her own people, a girl of two and a half at her side and a baby in her arms.

Agnes would never remember her father's face. Everything she knew of him she would learn from others. But she carried his mother's name all her life, and one day she would hand a name of his family on to a son of her own.

The valley under Tinto was where she would grow up. It was where her story would turn.

Chapter 3. The Cottages at Quothquan

Quothquan, Lanarkshire, 1891

The census of 1891 takes up the girls' story in their mother's village. Christina had died at Quothquan that February. Her name is on the Henderson family monument. When the census was taken on April 5, Mary was seven, Agnes five, and both were living with Christina's mother.

The census gave their address as Castle. A crofter, a mole catcher, a dressmaker, a stonebreaker and a farm servant were among the people recorded there. The census listed their grandmother, Jane Henderson, as fifty-six, born at Carnwath, and head of the household. Her daughter Jane was the dressmaker. Andrew was still at school.

Another young man lived with them. James Parker, eighteen and born at Biggar, was working as an agricultural laborer. Ten years earlier, when James was eight, the census had called him an adopted son. Mary and Agnes appear on the later return as Jane's granddaughters. Even on a census page, the differences in how the children had come into the house were visible.

A few entries away lived Ann Archibald, a widow. Her daughter was Mary Archibald. One son, Alexander, was a stonebreaker. The other, John, was working on a farm. Ten years later members of both families were still in the village, though in different households.

That spring and summer the two households lost people within days of one another. Jane Henderson died on June 8. John Archibald died on June 20, four days after his eighteenth birthday. The dates survive in the monumental inscriptions. By midsummer Agnes had lost her father, her mother and the grandmother in whose house the census had found her that April.

Chapter 4. The Knight of Shieldhill

Buckingham Palace, London, July 15, 1907

In the summer of 1907 the same place name appeared in two very different records. Agnes gave Shieldhill, Biggar, as part of her address when she registered her son. Later that month it appeared in the London Gazette beside the name of a new knight.

Nathaniel Dunlop had been born at Campbeltown. In 1847 he entered the Glasgow counting house of George Gillespie, whose business handled work for the Allan shipping line. In 1853 Dunlop joined the Allan firm itself. His career followed a route familiar to generations of Scottish emigrants, from Glasgow across the Atlantic to Canada. He came to run the shipping business and to chair the Clyde Navigation Trust.

The estate at Shieldhill, long held by the Chancellor family, came into his possession in 1898. The mansion incorporated an old stone tower. Its farms and cottages extended the estate far beyond the house itself.

On July 15, 1907, Edward the Seventh conferred the knighthood on Dunlop at Buckingham Palace. Among the other men knighted there that day were the painter Hubert von Herkomer, James Donaldson of the University of St. Andrews, and William Schwenck Gilbert, who had written the words to The Mikado. The Gazette published their names on July 26.

The honour belonged to Sir Nathaniel. The address belonged to a place where a great many people made their living. To follow Agnes, we have to leave the list of honours and look at the houses below his.

Chapter 5. Shieldhill

Quothquan, Lanarkshire, 1898–1906

By March 1901, the enumerator at Quothquan had names for the cottages. At Gean Tree lived Mary Archibald, a sewing mistress. Another household at Gean Tree consisted of James Grossart, an elderly road worker, and his granddaughter Marion. At Lime Tree Cottage the head of the house was Andrew Henderson.

Andrew was twenty-five and a groom. Local people later remembered him as a poet and a keeper of bees. His nieces were still together. Mary Brown was seventeen and Agnes was fifteen. James Parker, who had lived with their grandmother, appears again, this time as a boarder. He had become a gamekeeper.

The names tell us something about the village that an estate address alone cannot. Gean is a Scots name for the wild cherry. Lime Tree and Gean Tree were distinct addresses. A later history of the parish says Lime Tree Cottage became part of the house known as Geantree. The row once called Castle is known today as Huntfield Place. The census gives the order in which the houses were recorded. It would take more than the numbers on that page to say who shared a wall.

Mary Archibald's brother Alexander was now a domestic gardener. The census gave his address, and his wife Annie's, as Shieldhill. In August 1903 James Parker married Janet Crawford Stewart. He signed himself James Lawson Parker and gave his occupation and address as gamekeeper at Shieldhill, Biggar. By 1911 the Parkers had a home at Shieldhill Mains, with three children, John, Euphemia and Robert.

The Dunlops left their own marks on the parish. In 1903 Nathaniel and his daughter Ellen built Shieldhill Hall for evening worship and other gatherings. In 1911 Ellen helped establish a creamery at Thankerton, beside the railway station, where local farms could send their milk. She later helped found the Quothquan Women's Rural Institute. The hall, the creamery and the cottages all belonged to the history of the same small district.

Agnes appears in the records as a domestic servant. Her employer's name has yet to turn up. The address she gave was a cottage on the estate.

Chapter 6. Lizzie

Liberton, Edinburgh, 1874–1906

She was four years old when her mother sailed for the other side of the world without her.

Her name was Elizabeth Millar McDonald, and everyone called her Lizzie. She was born on May 31, 1874, at Liberton, on the southern edge of Edinburgh. Her mother, Ann McDonald, was about twenty.

Ann was her whole world. When Lizzie was two, her mother married a laborer named Robert Hope. And in 1878 New Zealand was paying the passage of working families who would come and settle its land. Ann and her new husband took it: three months under sail, halfway round the earth, to a place called Cross Creek, in the Wairarapa, that might as well have been the moon.

They took their new life with them.

They did not take Lizzie.

No record tells us how that morning went. No one wrote down whether Ann knelt and held her, or whether she slipped away while the child was asleep so there would be no scene. No one wrote down what she said, if she said anything at all. We know only that a four-year-old girl woke up in Scotland, and her mother was on a ship, and the ship was not coming back.

Picture her. Old enough to know her mother's face, her voice, the warm smell of her neck. Old enough to remember. Not old enough to understand. A four-year-old cannot understand an ocean. She cannot understand a new husband or a fresh start or a passage paid by a government on the far side of the world. She understands only this: Mummy was here, and now Mummy is gone, and nobody will say when she is coming home.

So she waits. That is what small children do. She listens for her mother's step on the stairs. She watches the door. She asks, and asks again, until the grown-ups run out of answers and change the subject. Every night she goes to sleep thinking that tomorrow, surely, tomorrow. And one day, without anyone telling her, she stops asking. That is the day a child learns the hardest lesson there is: that she can be left.

Out on the ocean, somewhere on that long voyage, Ann gave birth to a baby boy. He was born at sea in September 1878, and they named him for the ship. In New Zealand there would be more children after him: a whole family, brothers and sisters, a mother tucking them in at night. Lizzie would never meet a single one of them. Her mother lived another thirty-seven years. In all that time, as far as any record shows, she never came back for her daughter. Ann died at Cross Creek in January 1915, twelve thousand miles away, and in Dunfermline a grown woman of forty, a wife and a mother herself, was still the little girl who had been left behind.

Her grandparents caught her. John and Janet McDonald of Gilmerton were nearly fifty, with their own children grown, and they took the little girl into their house on the Main Road and raised her. In 1881 the census found her there, six years old, at school, written down as "Grandchild." But it was Janet McDonald who sat with her when she cried for her mother. It was Janet who was there in the morning. It was Janet who stayed.

At sixteen Lizzie went into service, scrubbing other people's floors, minding other people's babies, sleeping in a narrow room under someone else's roof. All her life she had belonged to a house without quite belonging to it.

Then, at twenty-five, she found someone who was hers.

On June 9, 1899, in Edinburgh, she married Robert Douglas, a steady, decent young man of twenty-three who worked at a sawmill and would soon be a skilled stonecutter, the son of a forester. On her wedding day her mother was twelve thousand miles away. When the registrar asked for the names of her parents, Lizzie did not give her mother's name. She gave the names of the two people who had stayed: John McDonald and Janet McDonald. Twenty-one years after she was left, she wrote her mother out of her life with the stroke of a pen.

The next spring, on April 29, 1900, she held a daughter of her own for the first time. Imagine that moment for a woman like Lizzie: the tiny weight in her arms, the small hand closing round her finger, and the promise she must have made in her heart. I will never leave you. Never. She named the baby Janet Page Douglas, for the grandmother who had caught her when her mother let go.

Then came a second little girl. They named her Isabella Gray Douglas, for Robert's mother.

Picture the flat in those days. Little Janet, barely walking, hauls herself up on the edge of the cradle on tiptoe to peer in at her sister, and pats the blanket with a fat hand, and says the baby's name the only way she can. In the evening Robert comes up the stairs from the sawmill with sawdust in his hair and goes straight to the cradle before he has his coat off, and the baby turns her head at the sound of his voice. Lizzie stands at the stove and watches the three of them, and for a little while, for the first time in her life, she has everything. A husband. Two daughters. A family that is hers, under her own roof, that no one can take away.

And they lost her.

That is almost all that is left of Isabella: a name, carried down through the family, and the memory that she died a baby. No record tells us how many weeks or months they had her. No record tells us what took her, whether it was the fever, or the cough, or one of the hundred things that took babies in the crowded apartments of Edinburgh in those years. But we know what it meant.

It meant a cradle by the bed with no one in it. It meant small clothes, washed and folded, that no one would ever wear again. It meant a coffin so small that one man could carry it, and a grave so short it hardly seemed a grave at all. It meant milk still coming for a baby who was not there. It meant waking in the night to listen for a cry that would never come, and lying awake till morning in the silence where it used to be.

Lizzie had been the child who was left behind. Now she was the mother whose child was taken. She had lost the mother she was born to, and the baby she was born to love. Everything life could take from a woman, it had taken from her, at both ends, before she was thirty.

And yet she did not close her heart. That is the miracle of Lizzie Douglas. After all of it, she still had more love in her than she had anywhere to put. Her arms were empty, and they were open.

Thirty miles away, in a cottage under Tinto, a young woman who had lost her own mother at five was about to face the hardest year of her life. Neither woman had ever heard of the other. But one baby boy was coming who would need a mother's arms, and one mother had arms that ached to be filled.

Part Two: The Year of the Child

Chapter 7. The Autumn of 1906

Quothquan, Lanarkshire, 1906

She was twenty years old, and she was on her own.

By the autumn of 1906 Agnes Brown was a grown woman. She had come through more than most people twice her age: a father she could not remember, a mother buried when she was five, a grandmother buried four months later. She had grown up in a house of men, under the bees and the cherry trees, and she had grown up capable. She could work from dawn until the lamps were lit. She could scrub a floor, black a grate, bake bread and keep a house. She had made her own way into service, earned her own wages, and kept her head up. People in the row had watched her grow from a frightened little orphan into a young woman who could take care of herself.

That summer had ended in a heat wave. In the first days of September the afternoons went above eighty degrees, and in the fields of the upper Clyde the oats stood cut and stooked in the heat, and the whole valley smelled of straw and dust. Then the weather broke. Gales came through. The nights turned cold. The leaves on the gean trees went red, then fell.

And somewhere in those weeks, as the year turned toward winter, Agnes began to realize that something was different.

At first it was only small things. A tiredness that would not lift, so that by midafternoon she could have lain down on the kitchen floor and slept. A queasiness in the mornings, at the smell of the porridge pot or the frying pan. A tenderness she did not understand. She told herself it was the change in the weather, or something she had eaten, or that she was run down. She told herself it would pass.

It did not pass.

Then the days went by, and the thing that should have come did not come. She counted. She counted again, lying awake in the dark, hoping she had made a mistake. And slowly, the way cold water rises, the truth came up around her until there was no getting out of it.

She was going to have a baby.

No one wrote down what Agnes felt that autumn. But any woman who has ever counted the days in the dark knows something of it. First the disbelief: it cannot be, not me, not now. Then the fear, sudden and total, like the floor dropping away. Her heart pounding. Her hands shaking. A hot wave of panic and then a cold one. What will happen to me? What will people say? What do I do?

And then, under the fear, something worse. The loneliness.

Because the first thing a frightened girl does, the very first thing, is run to her mother. She wants her mother's arms, her mother's voice saying it will be all right, we'll manage, I'm here. She wants someone who has been through it to tell her what is happening to her body and what is coming next and that she will not die of it. She wants to be held.

Agnes had no mother to run to. She had not had a mother since she was five years old. There was no one to whisper it to across a kitchen table, no one to tell her what the sickness meant or how long it would last, no one who could look at her and simply know. Her grandmother was in the churchyard. Her aunt had gone. Whoever else was under the roof at Lime Tree, there was no mother there. How could she tell them? How could she even find the words?

So she kept it inside. She went to work and did her work. She smiled when she was spoken to. She kept her secret close under her apron, and every night she lay in the dark with it, with nobody in the whole world who knew.

Think of what lay in front of her. She was twenty, unmarried, and a servant. In 1906 a servant girl in a family way could lose her place, and with it her wages and the roof over her head. She had no savings to speak of. She had no husband. She had no parents to take her in, and no grandmother. Everything she had built for herself, her place, her good name, her independence, all of it could be gone by the spring. And in a few months there would be no hiding it. Her body would tell the whole village.

And yet there was something else in those nights too, something she may not have dared to say even to herself. There was a child. A real child, growing under her heart. Her child. A little person who would need everything she did not have: a home, a father, a family, food on the table, a future. She knew better than anyone what it was to grow up without a mother and a father. She knew what it was to be the child in somebody else's house. Whatever happened, she did not want that for this baby. She wanted more for him than she had ever had.

That was the knot she could not untie. She loved this child already, and she could see no way to give him what she wanted him to have.

She was not a wicked girl. She was not a careless one. She was a young woman who had lost almost everyone, caught in circumstances far bigger than she was, in a world that had very little mercy for a girl in her position. Every choice in front of her was a hard one. Every road led somewhere painful.

But she could not carry it alone forever. As the nights drew in and the first frost whitened the fields, Agnes Brown knew she would have to tell someone, and soon.

She needed a woman. She needed someone who would not judge her.

And a few doors along the row, at Gean Tree, there was one.

Her name was Mary Archibald, and Agnes had known her since she was five years old.

Mary's brother, Alexander, knew where Agnes could turn. He and his family had grieved alongside hers in that terrible summer of 1891, when the row buried her grandmother and his own young brother within twelve days of each other, and by now he was the gardener at Shieldhill House, up the hill, a steady man everyone in the village knew. His sister Mary lived alone at Gean Tree, a few doors from Lime Tree. She earned her own living with her needle as a sewing mistress, she had raised a child of her own, and she knew what lay ahead. Alexander helped arrange for Agnes to stay with her.

It was the wisest thing Agnes ever did.

So she moved a few doors along, into Gean Cottage, where an older woman she trusted could look after her and see her through. Mary did far more than give her a bed. Through the winter of 1906 and into the spring of 1907, while the child grew, Mary Archibald kept her. At Gean Cottage Agnes had a roof, a fire, food on the table and the company of a woman who could tell her what to expect. Mary sat with her through the sickness and the waiting. On the long dark evenings the two of them sat by the fire with their needles and made ready, stitch by stitch, the little things a baby would need.

For the first time since she was five, Agnes was not alone.

Chapter 8. The Road to Edinburgh

Upper Clydesdale and Edinburgh, Spring 1907

It was thirty miles to Edinburgh. For Agnes Brown it might as well have been the other side of the world.

By the end of May 1907 her baby was only weeks away. She could not have the child at Gean Cottage, and she could not have it in the village, under the eyes of everyone she had ever known. The great maternity hospital of Scotland stood in Edinburgh, in Lauriston Place, and that was where she would go.

Picture her leaving. Mary Archibald walks with her to the door of Gean Cottage. Agnes has a small bag in her hand with everything she owns for the journey: a nightgown, a clean apron, a shawl, a few things for the baby that the two of them have sewn by the fire through the winter. She is heavy now, slow on her feet. She looks back once along the row, at Lime Tree, at the bee skeps in her uncle's garden, at the gean trees just finished with their blossom. Then she turns and sets off down the road.

It was a mile and a half to the station at Thankerton, across the Clyde. Every step of it she knew. She had walked this road all her life. She crossed the bridge over the river where she had played as a child, and Tinto stood behind her, red and enormous, the hill that had looked down on her every day she could remember. She did not know when she would see it again.

On the platform she counted out her fare: two shillings and more, close to half a week's wages, for a ticket to a place she had hardly ever been. Then the train came in, smoke and steam and the scream of the brakes, and she climbed up into a third-class carriage, and found a seat by the window, and put her hand on her belly, and the train began to move.

North up the Clyde valley it ran, past the green meadows and the hedgerows, the farms where she knew the names of the families, the fields where the young oats were just coming up. Every mile took her farther from everyone she had in the world.

What would they say at home? Had anyone guessed? Would they talk about her at the church door on Sunday? Would there be a home to come back to?

At Carstairs Junction the line divided, one way for Glasgow, one for Edinburgh, and her carriage swung east and began to climb. The country opened up into high moorland, brown and wide and empty, with curlews calling over it and the wind bending the grass. They passed Carnwath, a gray village on the moor. Her grandmother had been born there. Her grandmother, who had taken her in when her mother died, and who had died herself four months later. Agnes looked out at the place and thought of her, and of her mother, and of how much she wanted a mother now.

What would it be like? Would it hurt as much as people said? Women died having babies. Everyone knew someone. Who would be with her? Who would hold her hand?

The train ran on across the top of the moor, past lonely little stations with names like Auchengray and Cobbinshaw, where a long gray reservoir lay beside the line, flat and cold under the sky. There was nothing out there but water and heather and sheep. She had never felt so small.

And the baby. She felt the baby move. It turned and pushed, and she laid her hand over the place and felt the small hard shape of a heel or an elbow under her palm. In a few weeks she would see its face. Would it be a boy or a girl? Would it look like her? What name would she give it? And then the thought she had pushed away all winter came back, as it always did: and then what? Where would they live? How would she feed him? Who would keep him while she worked? What kind of life could she give him, a girl with no husband, no mother, no money, nothing but her two hands?

The moor fell away behind them. The train came down off the high ground into the rich farmland of Midlothian, past Harburn and Midcalder, and away to the south rose the long blue ridge of the Pentland Hills. The farms here were bigger, the fields greener, the villages closer together. Then houses began to crowd the line, and chimneys, and smoke. At Slateford the train ran beside the canal where it crossed the Water of Leith high on its stone arches, and then there were streets, and more streets, and walls of tall gray apartment blocks, and the city swallowed her up.

The train ran into Princes Street Station under its great roof of glass and iron, and stopped.

She stepped down into a noise like nothing she had ever heard. Hundreds of people, all in a hurry. Porters shouting. Engines hissing. Doors slamming all along the train. Out in the street the trams clanged past, and cabs and carts and motor cars, and over everything, on its black rock high above Princes Street, stood Edinburgh Castle. Three hundred thousand people lived in this city, and not one of them knew her name.

She picked up her bag and asked the way to Lauriston Place.

It was the better part of a mile, uphill, through streets of tall stone buildings that shut out the sky. She walked it slowly, stopping when she had to, a country girl in her best clothes, heavy with child, alone in the capital. At last she came to a tall stone building with crow-stepped gables and a square tower at the corner, like an old Scottish town house, at number 79: the Royal Maternity Hospital.

She stood at the door for a moment. Behind her lay Quothquan, the bees, the cherry trees, Mary Archibald, everything she knew. In front of her lay the thing she was most afraid of in the world.

Agnes Brown was twenty-one years old. She took a breath, and went in.

Chapter 9. Born at Nine O'Clock

Edinburgh, June 14, 1907

Friday, June 14, 1907, was a mild, gray day in Edinburgh, overcast and still. Agnes Brown would remember it for the rest of her life.

The ward was long and high, with rows of iron beds and tall windows, and the women in the beds were women like her: servants, farm girls, shop girls, most of them young, many of them alone. Pupil midwives in starched aprons moved between the beds. Students came and went. Nobody there knew where she came from, and nobody asked very much. For a girl who had spent the winter hiding, there was a strange relief in that.

The pains came.

At first they were far apart, a tightening low down that came and went, and she could lie still and breathe and wait for it to pass. Then they came closer. Then harder. Each one gathered like a wave, rose until there was nothing in the world but the pain, and then slowly let her go, and she lay gasping in the gap before the next one came. The hours went by. The light outside the tall windows began to change.

Picture her. Twenty-one years old, sweat in her hair, gripping the iron rail of the bed. A stranger's hand on her forehead, a stranger's voice telling her she was doing well. Every woman in the world wants her mother at that hour, and Agnes wanted hers. She had not seen her mother's face since she was five. She called for no one, because there was no one to call. No husband paced the corridor outside. No sister waited with a shawl. Thirty miles away the people she loved went about their evening without knowing that this was the night.

It went on and on. She thought she could not do it anymore. She did it anyway.

The long June evening drew down. Outside, over the city, the sun was going down behind the clouds. And at nine o'clock, as the last of the daylight faded from the windows, with one last terrible effort, her son was born.

For a moment there was silence. Then he cried.

A thin, furious, wonderful cry, the cry of a new person announcing himself to the world. Agnes heard it and something broke open in her. Someone said, "It's a boy." They wiped him and wrapped him and laid him on her chest, and for the first time she saw his face.

He was red and crumpled and perfect. He had a little damp fuzz of hair and a tiny mouth that worked at the air. His eyes opened, dark and unfocused, and seemed to look straight up at her. She touched his cheek with one finger. His whole hand closed around it and held on.

After the whole long, frightened, lonely winter, here he was. Hers.

Overnight a drizzle came on and pattered against the windows. Agnes lay in the dark ward with her son in the crook of her arm and listened to him breathe.

Mothers were usually kept about ten days. They were the only ten days Agnes and her son would ever have entirely to themselves.

She fed him. She learned the weight of him, the warm milky smell of his head, the little sounds he made in his sleep. She learned how he liked to be held and what made him settle. She counted his fingers and his toes. She studied his face, trying to find in it her father, whom she could not remember, and her mother, whom she could. In the long nights she rocked him and whispered to him, and nobody knew what she told him, and nobody ever will.

And all the time, under the love, the question she had carried for nine months sat beside the bed and waited. What now? Where would they go? How would she keep him, a servant girl with no husband, no mother, no money, nothing but her two hands? She would look down at him asleep against her, so small and so trusting, and the love and the fear came up together so hard she could hardly breathe.

She had twenty-one days to register his birth. On the twentieth day, she signed as his mother. The name in the register was Lawrence Brown.

Every one of those days counted.

Chapter 10. His First Name

Edinburgh, July 4, 1907

She had twenty-one days to register him. She waited until the twentieth.

Nineteen days she had him without a word of it written down anywhere. Nineteen days when he was simply hers, a baby with no name on any paper, held and fed and rocked and sung to, belonging to no one in the world but her. Why did she wait so long? No one can say. Perhaps she was too weak at first. Perhaps there was no money for the tram, or no one to ask. Or perhaps, every morning, she looked at him and could not bring herself to do it yet, because once it was written down it would all be real.

But the law was the law. On Thursday, July 4, 1907, the twentieth day, she went.

It was a raw, gray morning for July, cold enough for a shawl, with a wind off the sea and showers blowing across the city. The registrar for the district of St Giles kept his office at 24 Forrest Road, a short walk from the hospital, up past the old Greyfriars churchyard. Picture her going up that street: twenty-one years old, still sore, still pale, three weeks from childbirth, her son wrapped against the wind in her arms. Other women walked to that office with a husband at their side, or a mother, or a sister. Agnes walked alone.

Inside, behind a desk, sat the registrar, a man in a dark suit with a pen in his hand and the great ruled book open in front of him. He asked his questions, one by one, the way he asked them of everyone, and wrote the answers down in his neat clerk's hand.

The child's name?

She had thought about it for months. She gave him the name of her father's elder brother, her uncle Lawrence, a man who had married, raised a family and made a good life for himself. It was a name with hope in it. Lawrence Brown.

When born, and where? The fourteenth of June, nine o'clock in the evening, at the Royal Maternity Hospital. Male.

And then the next question. The name and occupation of the father.

Agnes gave no answer.

The registrar's pen moved on, and the space was left empty. In the column where every other child's father was written down, on every other line of that page, his name, his trade, the date and place of the marriage, there was nothing for her son. Just a blank, ruled lines with nothing between them. It is still there today, more than a century later, in the register in Edinburgh: an empty space where a father should be.

Think about what that column meant to her. She had no father; he had died when she was a baby. She had no mother; she had buried her at five. And now she sat in a stranger's office and watched a man write down that her son had no father either. Three generations of loss on one page. She had grown up knowing exactly what it was to have no one, and she was looking at the proof, in black ink, that her child was starting his life the same way.

How does a young woman bear a moment like that? How does she sit there with her baby in her arms, alone, in a strange city, with nobody in the world to turn to and say, What do I do now? She was twenty-one. She had no husband, no parents, no home of her own, no money, and a son three weeks old. Every door she could see led somewhere hard.

Name of the mother? Agnes Brown. Occupation? Domestic servant.

Her home? She did not give Lime Tree, the house she had grown up in. She gave Gean Cottage, Shieldhill, Biggar, Mary Archibald's house, the one place in the world where someone had taken her in and asked her no hard questions. It was the closest thing she had to a mother's door.

Then the registrar turned the great book round to her and held out the pen. In the column for the informant she signed it herself, in her own hand: Agnes Brown, Mother.

Mother. She was somebody's mother now, and she had written it down for the whole world to see.

The registrar blotted the page and signed beneath. It was done. Her son had a name, written in the books of Scotland forever: Lawrence Brown, entry 839.

She stood, and gathered him up, and went out again into the wind and the rain on Forrest Road. She had given him the only things she had to give: his life, his name and her own.

What she could not give him was everything else. And she knew it.

Chapter 11. The Missing Years

July 1907 – April 1911

On the fourth of July, 1907, Agnes Brown walked out of the registrar's office on Forrest Road. And with that, her son vanishes from the record.

From that day until the spring of 1911, there is nothing. We have found no record that shows where he went that summer, where he lived, or who took care of him. No letter survives. No photograph. No story has come down in the family. For almost four years, the boy is simply gone from the paper trail, and what happened to him in those years, no one knows.

It is important to be honest about that, because it is the deepest silence in this whole story.

We know only the edges of it. On one side, a young mother signs her name in a register and gives her home as Gean Cottage, Shieldhill, Biggar. On the other side, in April 1911, a census taker in Edinburgh finds a boy of three living as the son of another family, under another name. Everything in between is dark.

Did Agnes take him home to Gean Cottage and to Mary Archibald? Perhaps. That was the address she gave. Did she keep him for a few weeks, or a few months, or longer? Did someone else nurse him while she went back to work? Was he in Quothquan, or Edinburgh, or somewhere else entirely? When did he go to the Douglases, and how, and who brought him? Did Agnes find them, or did they find her? No one alive can say. The records are silent, and the family has no account of it.

What we do know is where Agnes went. By the summer of 1911 she had put on a nurse's uniform in Edinburgh and was starting a new life. And we know where her son went. By the spring of 1911 he was living with Lizzie Douglas in an apartment at Tollcross, and he had a new name.

Somewhere in those missing years, whenever it happened and however it happened, a young mother let go of her son.

We cannot see that moment. We do not know how old he was when he went to live with the Douglases, or whether it happened in a cottage or a station or at a stranger's door. But we know what Agnes had been carrying since the autumn of 1906: no husband, no parents, a servant's wages, and a love for her child that wanted more for him than she could give. However it came about, letting him go must have been the hardest thing she ever did. No mother does it lightly. No mother ever forgets.

And somewhere on the other side of that moment, a woman who had once been the child left behind opened her door.

Part Three: Another Name

Chapter 12. The Douglases

Edinburgh, by April 1911

The woman who opened her door was Lizzie Douglas.

No one knows the day he came to her, or how old he was, or who brought him. But think about who was waiting on the other side of that door.

Lizzie, who had been left behind at four when her mother sailed away. Lizzie, who had waited at the window and asked and asked until she stopped asking. Lizzie, who had been caught and raised by her grandmother. Lizzie, who had held her own baby Isabella and lost her, and folded away the little clothes, and lain awake in the silence where a cry used to be. For years her arms had ached. And now there was a little boy in her house who needed a mother.

She knew exactly what a child like that could feel. She had been that child.

So, whenever he came, she did for him what her grandmother had done for her. She took him in. She held him when he cried, and she did not tell him to stop. Picture the first nights: a small boy waking in the dark in a strange room, and Lizzie coming to him, sitting on the edge of the bed, stroking his hair, singing to him until he slept. And then the night, sooner or later, when he woke and called out, and the name he called was hers.

Lizzie Douglas knew better than anyone alive that a child who has been left can be loved whole again. She set out to prove it.

She gave him a bed and a place at the table, a big sister named Janet, a father named Robert, and a name: Douglas. The Brown went with him too, as a middle initial, B, so that the name his mother had given him would stay with him all his life. And she loved him, fully and for the rest of her life, as if he had been born to her. A woman who had been taken in took in a child.

Whatever came before, no story of it ever came down in the family. Lizzie and Robert were the parents he knew.

On census night, Sunday, April 2, 1911, the enumerator climbed the stairs of an apartment building at 16 Lochrin Place, at Tollcross in Edinburgh, half a mile from the hospital where the boy had been born. He wrote down Elizabeth Douglas, wife, thirty-six; Janet, daughter, ten; and below them, in the column that asked each person's relation to the head of the family, one word.

Laurence B. Douglas, three. Son.

It is the first time any record finds him since the day his mother registered his birth. He is three years old, he has a new family and a new name, and he is the son of the house.

Two women had each lost almost everything. One had let go of the only thing she had left in the world, so that he could have more than she had. The other had been given the one thing she had longed for most. And between them stood a little boy with a new name, who would carry one letter of the old one, B, for the rest of his life.

Chapter 13. Agnes Marries

Morningside, Edinburgh, 1910–1919

She went looking for a new life, and she found one. But on her wedding day, the past was waiting for her across a desk.

By the summer of 1911 Agnes Brown had put on a uniform. She was a nurse at the Royal Edinburgh Asylum in Morningside, the great mental hospital on the high ground on the south side of the city, a small town of its own behind its walls, with gardens and farms and long sandstone wards. It was hard, honest work: long days on her feet, a long cotton dress, a starched white apron and cap, a ring of keys at her belt. It gave her a bed, a wage, training, and something she had not had in years: a future that was hers alone.

Picture her in those first months. Busy from dawn until dark, so busy there was hardly time to think, which may have been exactly what she wanted. But at night, in the nurses' quarters, when the lamps went out, there was time. And in the dark she must have thought of her son, wherever he was now, and whether he was happy.

Then she met Thomas McKenzie.

He was an attendant at the same hospital, a young man from a seafaring family, the son of a coastguardsman. He was four years younger than she was, twenty-one to her twenty-five, and he had lost his own mother. Perhaps that was part of what drew them together: two young people who knew what it was to grow up with a hole where a mother should be. They worked the same wards, walked the same paths between the buildings, and sometime in that summer of 1911 they fell in love.

Did she tell him about her son? No record says. Perhaps she told him everything, on some long walk under the trees, and he took her hand and said it made no difference. Perhaps she told him nothing, and carried it alone, the way she had carried so much. Either way, she must have wondered: if he knew all of it, would he still want me?

He wanted her. On Monday, August 28, 1911, they went down into the Old Town to be married.

It was not a church wedding. There was no organ, no minister, no bride's father to walk her down an aisle; she had no father. It was a Scottish marriage by declaration: the two of them would declare before witnesses that they took each other as husband and wife, a sheriff's warrant would make it lawful, and the registrar would write it into the books. A fellow attendant from the hospital and a coachman came with them to stand as witnesses. Agnes had no mother to help her dress that morning, no sister to fix her hair. She did it herself, as she had done everything herself.

They climbed the stairs at 34 Chambers Street and went in to the registrar.

And there he was.

Behind the desk, with the great ruled book open in front of him and the pen in his hand, sat R. D. Robertson, the registrar for St Giles. The same man. Four years earlier, almost to the season, Agnes had sat across a desk from this same man, three weeks out of childbirth, with her son in her arms, and watched him leave the father's column empty. She had watched him write Lawrence Brown into the books of Scotland forever. She had signed his book: Agnes Brown, Mother.

Now here she was again, in her best dress, with a young man at her side who was about to become her husband.

Think of what went through her in that moment. The room tilting. Her heart slamming in her chest. The blood draining out of her face. Did he know her? Would he remember? Would he look up from his book, frown, and say: Miss Brown? Haven't you been here before? And with what? Would everything she had buried come up out of the ground in front of Thomas, in front of the witnesses, in front of the whole new life she was about to begin?

And at the very same time, under the fear, the other thing. The memory, so sharp it hurt. The weight of the baby in her arms that day in July. His warm head under her chin. His tiny fingers. The pen scratching. Lawrence Brown. She had been his mother before this very man. And now she was standing here without him, about to become someone else.

Two feelings, pulling her in two directions at once. Terror that the past would be seen. Grief that it was gone.

R. D. Robertson looked up, and asked his questions, the way he asked them of everyone.

He did not know her. How could he? He had written thousands of names since that summer: births, marriages, deaths, a whole city's worth of joy and sorrow passing across his desk. One more young woman from the country was nothing to him. He dipped his pen and wrote, in the same neat clerk's hand.

Thomas McKay McKenzie, attendant, twenty-one, of West House, Morningside.

Agnes McGarth Brown, nurse, twenty-five, of the same place.

The father of the bride? George Brown. And when she gave her father's trade, she gave him a step up he had not lived to reach: not plowman, but farm grieve, the man who runs a farm. Deceased. Her mother, Christina Brown, maiden surname Henderson. Deceased.

Then he turned the book round and held out the pen, and for the second time in her life Agnes signed R. D. Robertson's register. Four years before, she had signed it as a mother. Now she signed it as a bride. Her hand may have shaken. She signed her full name, the name her father had given her from the hills of Tweedsmuir: Agnes McGarth Brown.

It was done. She walked out of that office into the August sunshine as Mrs. Thomas McKenzie. Behind her, in the same registrar's books, on page 280 of the births for 1907, her son's entry lay where it had always been. No one in the room but Agnes knew that the two pages belonged to the same story.

The next spring, at ten in the morning on May 6, 1912, in a flat at 9 Stewart Terrace, she gave birth to a daughter.

Think of that moment for her. A baby laid on her chest, warm and new and crying, and every part of her remembering the last time. The same smell. The same small fingers. The same impossible love. She named the little girl Mary Christina McKenzie, for her sister and for her mother. This child she would keep. This child she would raise, and see grow, and never have to let go. And yet holding her must have brought it all back: the boy who was not there. The first child. The one she had carried through that frightened winter.

In August 1914, in the very month the war began, a second daughter came: Jean Henderson McKenzie, carrying the name of Agnes's mother's people.

The war emptied the hospital of its men. In December 1915 Thomas McKenzie went to enlist. On his army papers he listed his family: his wife, Agnes McKenzie, and his two daughters, Mary, born May 6, 1912, and Jean, born August 1914.

There was no son on the form.

There never would be. Across the water, in Dunfermline, a boy of eight was walking to school with a different name, holding a different woman's hand. On opposite shores of the Forth, Agnes raised her daughters and Lizzie raised her son, and the boy who belonged to both of them went on growing up with no idea, as far as anyone knows, that the mother who bore him was only a few miles away.

Part Four: Dunfermline

Chapter 14. Lawrence Douglas

Dunfermline, Fife, 1912

In the summer of 1912 the Douglases crossed the water to begin again in a new town, and the boy went with them.

They took a flat at 36 Victoria Terrace, Dunfermline—a long run of four-story sandstone row houses with bay windows and tall chimneys. That first evening, perhaps, he stood at the glass, five years old, nose to the pane, studying a town he had never seen.

It was a good town for a boy. Dunfermline was an ancient seat of Scottish kings, where Robert the Bruce lies buried in the abbey, a town of linen mills and coal pits set on a hill above the water. Andrew Carnegie—the weaver's son who became one of the wealthiest men on earth—had been born there, and he had given his birthplace a magnificent public library, public swimming pools, and an entire wooded glen of gardens and lawns, Pittencrieff Park, where peacocks paraded across the grass. Three miles to the south, on the shore of the Forth, thousands of men were raising an immense new naval dockyard at Rosyth to confront the German fleet across the North Sea. No one yet knew how soon it would be needed.

On Monday, August 19, 1912, the boy was enrolled at the Queen Anne School.

Picture the morning.

Victoria Terrace, early. Mist still hangs over the Forth below the town. A door opens on the stairwell of number 36, and a small boy steps out into the gray light, scrubbed pink, dressed in his best: short pants and long wool stockings, a sweater buttoned to the neck beneath a wide white collar, a cap pulled low over hair cut short the night before, and boots polished to a shine. Lizzie straightens his collar one last time. Then Robert Douglas takes his hand, and they start up the hill.

The hobnailed boots ring on the cobblestones. The boy takes two steps for every one of Robert's. He says nothing. Somewhere beneath the new sweater sits a knot in his stomach—the same knot every child on earth has felt on the first morning of school—and it tightens with every street they climb.

They turn the last corner, and there it is.

The Queen Anne School looms over the street like a fortress: a tall, dark stone building three times the height of anything he has ever stood beside, its stepped gables cut against the sky, its rows of high windows staring down at him. The boy stops. Robert waits, then gives his hand a gentle tug, and they go on.

Then the sound reaches him.

Since at least the spring of 1911, his world had been a small flat, a few adult voices, an older sister, and the tick of a clock—and before that, years no one recorded. Now the yard is a roar. Hundreds of children shout at once. Boys chase one another in circles; hobnailed boots clatter on the stone; girls skip rope to their chants; a ball smacks the wall; someone is crying, someone laughing, and above it all a teacher swings a brass hand bell that clangs and clangs. It comes at him from every direction—too loud, too fast, too many faces. He presses against Robert's leg and grips his hand with both of his.

They cross the yard together. Robert pushes open the great wooden doors. They swing shut behind them with a deep, heavy thud.

And then, silence.

Inside, the clamor of the yard falls abruptly away, muffled behind the doors. The hallway runs ahead, long and high and dim. The floorboards are bare, worn pale down the center by thousands of boots before his, and every step echoes off the walls and returns to them. The air smells of chalk dust, coal smoke, and floor polish. From behind a closed door, an entire class recites its multiplication tables in a single voice. The boy looks up at Robert. Robert looks down and nods.

In a room off the hallway, the clerk opened the infant admission register and entered the boy.

Name: Lawrence Douglas. Date of birth: June 14, 1907. Parent or guardian: Robert Douglas. Address: 36 Victoria Terrace. Previous school: none. And in the column for the child's relationship to his guardian, the clerk wrote a single word—the term that era used for a child taken into a family and raised as its own.

The boy was five years, two months, and five days old. Five years after a registrar in Edinburgh wrote Lawrence Brown, a school clerk in Dunfermline wrote Lawrence Douglas.

Two days later his sister Janet, twelve, started at the same school, in the department for the older children. Perhaps, from then on, the two of them climbed the hill together each morning—the tall girl and the small boy, the daughter born to Robert and Lizzie and the son they had taken in—and in the yard, if any older boy so much as looked at him sideways, he had a sister to answer to.

He was Lawrence Douglas now, in a new town, with a family of his own. For a little while, the world held steady under his feet. Down at Rosyth, the hammering on the great dockyard went on day and night.

Chapter 15. A Boy in Dunfermline

Dunfermline, Fife, 1912–1916

He grew up three miles from the greatest warships in the world, and for a boy of seven, nothing could have been more thrilling. It took him years to understand that it was also terrifying.

In August 1914, when Britain went to war with Germany, Laurence was seven. That summer his big sister Janet, who had turned fourteen in April, left school to go out to work. And across the water in Edinburgh, in the same month, the mother he could not remember gave birth to another daughter, a half-sister he may never have known he had.

Then, in December, the battlecruisers came.

Picture a small boy standing on the hill above Rosyth on a cold winter morning, with his breath smoking and his hands jammed in his pockets, staring down at the estuary. There they lay at anchor in the gray water: Admiral Beatty's battlecruisers, the fastest, most powerful warships ever built, long and gray and bristling with guns, their funnels trailing smoke, their names on every boy's lips. Lion. Tiger. Princess Royal. Queen Mary. Their great guns could throw a shell farther than a boy could walk in an afternoon. To a seven-year-old they were the most magnificent things in the world.

For the next four years Dunfermline lived beside the war. Sailors filled the streets on shore leave, in their bell-bottoms and round caps, and small boys followed them about and begged for buttons and cap ribbons. At night searchlights swept across the sky over the Forth, long white fingers feeling for enemy airships, and a boy could lie in bed and watch them sliding over the ceiling. At school the children knitted socks and scarves for the soldiers, collected pennies for the troops, and wrote letters to men at the front. In the playground they played at war, Britain against the Germans, with sticks for rifles.

In January 1916, at eight, he moved up from the infants into the big school, Queen Anne Primary. By then the family had moved to a new flat at 5 Foundry Street, closer to the middle of the town. It was a street of working people, and a short walk up the hill stood the place that would shape the rest of his life.

It was Andrew Carnegie's library, the very first of the more than two thousand public libraries Carnegie would give to towns around the world. Over its door were carved the words "Let There Be Light." Inside, free to any child with a ticket, were shelves and shelves of books: Stevenson and Scott, pirates and castaways, Highland outlaws and buried treasure, every adventure a boy could dream of. Laurence loved to read, all his life. It began here, on a hard chair under the tall windows of Carnegie's library, with the rain on the glass and a book open on his knees.

He was a quiet boy, by every account of the man he became, and books were a place where a quiet boy could go anywhere. Kidnapped. Treasure Island. Boys who crossed oceans and came home rich.

Then the war came close.

On the evening of May 30, 1916, the battlecruisers slipped their moorings and steamed out of the Forth, one after another, past the Forth Bridge and out to sea. Two days later the news came back: a great battle off the coast of Denmark, at Jutland, the biggest sea battle of the war. On June 2 the ships came home.

Not all of them. Queen Mary did not come home. Indefatigable did not come home. They had blown up in the battle and gone down in minutes with almost every man aboard. More than six thousand British sailors were dead. The ships that did come back crept up the Forth to Rosyth with their sides torn open, black with smoke, their decks scarred, carrying their wounded. In Dunfermline and all along the Forth, families who had waved to those ships went down to the shore to find out who was alive.

A boy of eight who had stood on the hill and worshipped those ships learned that spring what they were really for.

But that spring he had already learned something more. The war was not only something that happened at sea, to sailors, far away. Two months before Jutland, it had come in the night to the city where he was born.

Chapter 16. Fire Over Edinburgh

Edinburgh and Leith, April 2–3, 1916

It was a Sunday night, and the town was asleep.

No one recorded what the Douglases saw or heard that night. But picture it. Sunday, April 2, 1916. In the flat at 5 Foundry Street the lamp was out, the fire banked down, the dishes put away. Robert and Lizzie had gone to bed. In the little room, a boy of eight lay under the blankets in the dark, half asleep, listening to the ordinary sounds of the night: the tick of the clock, a dog somewhere down the street, the wind off the Forth worrying at the window.

What he did not know was that, out over the North Sea, four German airships were coming.

They were Zeppelins: monsters of the sky, each one longer than a city street, great gray cigars of silk and aluminum filled with hydrogen, droning across the dark at the height of a mountain, too high for searchlights to hold and too high for guns to reach. Inside their cabins, men in leather coats and fur gloves leaned over their maps by the glow of shaded lamps. Their orders were to find the naval dockyard at Rosyth and the Forth Bridge, and to bomb them. Rosyth was three miles from the boy's bed.

The war had been going on for nearly two years, and in all that time it had happened somewhere else: in France, at sea, in the newspapers, in the letters from men at the front. For a boy of eight it was battleships to wave at and sailors to follow down the street. It had never come in through the roof. Nobody in Scotland believed it could.

Then, somewhere toward midnight, there was a sound.

A dull, heavy thud, far away, felt as much as heard, like a door slammed in a house down the street. Then another. The window glass gave a small shiver in its frame.

He froze.

Every muscle in his body locked. He lay rigid in the dark, eyes wide open, not breathing, his heart thudding so hard he could hear it in his ears, waiting to understand what he had heard. A boy's mind goes very fast and very slow at the same time in a moment like that. Thunder? No, there was no storm. The guns? Were the ships firing? Was it the Germans? Were they here?

Another thud. Louder. Then a long silence that was worse than the noise, because he did not know when the next one would come.

He heard Robert's feet hit the floor in the next room. Low voices. Lizzie saying something sharp and frightened. The scrape of the curtain being pulled back. And then Robert's voice, quiet and strange, a voice the boy had never heard him use before.

"Edinburgh's burning."

He crept out of bed and went to the window, and Lizzie pulled him in against her side. Away to the south, across the black water of the Forth, the sky was glowing. A dull red light, low on the horizon where Leith and Edinburgh lay, pulsing and flaring, as if someone had opened the door of a furnace on the far side of the world. Long white fingers of searchlights swept back and forth across the sky above it, searching, crossing, searching. And under it all came the sound, rolling across the water: thud, and thud, and a pause, and then a bigger one that made the glass hum.

It went on for more than an hour.

That was the worst of it, afterward, when he tried to remember: how long it lasted. An hour is nothing to a grown man. To a boy of eight standing in the dark at a window in his nightshirt, with his feet freezing on the floorboards and his mother's arm tight around his shoulders, it was forever. Some of the explosions were so faint he was not sure he had heard them at all. Others came so hard and sudden that he jumped and gripped the window ledge. There was no rhythm to it. That was what made it unbearable. He could not brace himself, because he never knew when the next one was coming, or how big it would be, or whether it would be closer.

Because that was the thought that went round and round in his head, the one he did not dare say out loud: What if they come here next? The dockyard was right there. The warships were right there. Everybody knew the Germans wanted Rosyth. What if the next bang was not over there, across the water, but here, on this street, on this roof, on this room?

And under the fear, the other questions, the ones a child asks and no grown-up can answer. Why? Why would anybody do this? What did the people in Edinburgh ever do to them? He did not understand anything about the Kaiser or empires or why great nations went to war. He was eight. All he knew was that somewhere up in the dark there were men he had never met, who had never met him, who were dropping fire on sleeping houses full of people who had done nothing to them, and that nobody could stop them. The grown-ups could not stop them. The great warships in the Forth could not stop them. The whole world he trusted, his house, his town, his father standing at the window, had turned out to be made of glass.

Across the water, this is what was happening.

The airship called L14 had come up the Forth in the dark and found the docks at Leith a little before half past eleven. For the next hour and a quarter she crept across the sleeping city dropping bombs as she went. A whisky warehouse on the wharf at Leith took a direct hit and went up in a pillar of flame that lit the whole sky: that was the glow the boy saw. An engineer was killed in his bed. In Bonnington, a little boy of one died in his cot.

Then, just before midnight, the airship swung south over the New Town toward the Old, and a bomb came through the roof of a house at 39 Lauriston Place.

Lauriston Place. The street where he was born. The Royal Maternity Hospital stood at number 79, a short way along from the house that was hit. Somewhere behind those walls, that night, women lay in the same iron beds where his mother had lain, with their babies beside them, listening to the bombs come closer. A young gunner, home from the war and invalided out of the army, was standing in the street nearby when the bomb went off. A fragment struck him down. He had survived the war, and he died of his wound in hospital.

A little later the deadliest bomb of the night fell outside an apartment building in Marshall Street, where men and boys had crowded into the entryway. Six of them died there. The youngest was five years old: the age Laurence had been when Robert walked him up the hill to school.

By the time L14 turned for home, she had dropped forty-four bombs and had not hit a single military target. Thirteen or fourteen people were dead, and two dozen more were hurt. No other German attack on Scottish soil in the whole of the war killed so many.

And in Morningside, on the south side of the city, in the flat on Balcarres Street that she had given as her home that winter, a young mother named Agnes McKenzie would have held her two little daughters in the dark, Mary, not yet four, and Jean, a baby, and listened to the same explosions coming closer across the rooftops, and watched the same red sky.

Two people, on two sides of the water, a mother and the son she had given away, both awake in the dark at the same hour, both staring at the same burning sky, both terrified, and neither of them knowing that the other was there.

Toward one in the morning, the sounds stopped. The airship slid away east into the dark over Dunbar and out to sea. The glow over Leith burned on for hours. In the flat on Foundry Street, Lizzie finally took the boy back to bed and sat with him until he slept.

He did not forget that night. Nobody who lived through it ever did. It was the night a boy of eight learned that the world could come for you in the dark, out of nowhere, for no reason you could understand, and that the people who loved you most could only hold you and wait for it to be over.

Chapter 17. The House on Foundry Street

Dunfermline, Fife, 1919–1926

The war did not take Robert Douglas. Something older did.

He had had lupus since he was a boy, the kind of tuberculosis that eats at the skin, and he had lived with it all his life, working the stone machines, coming home with the dust in his clothes. But in his last year it reached his lungs. Tuberculosis was the great killer of working Scotland, and there was only one treatment for it: rest, fresh air, and separation. Patients were sent away from their families to sanatoriums, so they would not spread it to their children.

So Robert was taken away from Foundry Street, across the Forth to Woodburn Sanatorium, on Canaan Lane, in Morningside, on the south side of Edinburgh. Picture the boy at the door, twelve years old, watching the man who had walked him to school on his first day go down the stairs for the last time.

Morningside. Of all the places in Scotland. The quiet streets where, only four years earlier, a nurse named Agnes McKenzie had given her address on her husband's army papers as Balcarres Street, less than a mile from Canaan Lane. The man who had raised her son went there to die, a short walk from where the woman who had borne him had made her home. Neither of them could have known.

Robert Douglas died at Woodburn at half past seven on the morning of Tuesday, September 9, 1919. He was forty-three. That same day Lizzie went to the registrar and gave her husband's name, his trade, stone machineman, and her own condition, widow, for the first time.

Think of Lizzie. Left by her mother at four. Her baby Isabella taken. And now her husband, the steady, decent man who had married her and stood beside her and taken in another woman's child. At forty-five she was alone again, with a daughter of nineteen and a boy of twelve, and no man's wage coming into the house.

And think of Laurence. Twelve years old, and the only father he had ever known was gone. For the second time in his life, a parent had been taken from him, and this time he was old enough to understand it, and to remember it for the rest of his life.

The house on Foundry Street went quiet. Then it went to work. Lizzie took a job as a school cleaner. Janet worked as a clerk at the Co-operative Society. And the boy, the reader, the quiet one who lived in Carnegie's library, did what boys did in houses without a father's wage.

On Friday, October 8, 1920, at thirteen years and four months, he left school for good. The leaving age was fourteen, but the education authority could let a boy go early if his family needed his wages, and the register gives the reason in four words: "Granted Exemption by E.A." There would be no more school for him. He was a working man now, at thirteen.

His first job was carrying groceries.

When the census taker came round on Sunday, June 19, 1921, five days after Laurence turned fourteen, he found the three of them still in three rooms at 5 Foundry Street. Elizabeth Douglas, head of the house now, widow, school cleaner. Janet, twenty-one, a clerk at the Co-operative. And Lawrence, son, fourteen: message boy for a grocer, McClelland and Company.

The census asked one more question about every child under fifteen: were the child's parents living? Beside his name, in the enumerator's hand, are two letters.

F. D. Father dead.

So the boy who had loved books went out every morning in a cap and an apron with a big basket on his arm, or pushing a heavy delivery bicycle up the steep streets of Dunfermline, carrying flour and tea and sugar and soap to other people's kitchen doors. He ran messages in the rain. He learned the shop: how to weigh out a pound of butter by eye, cut cheese with a wire, fold a paper bag so it held, add up a bill in his head faster than the customer could, and always, always be polite. It was honest work, and he was good at it. Year by year he rose from message boy to shop boy to grocer's assistant, and by his twenties he could call himself a grocer. Every week his wages went into the house on Foundry Street, into Lizzie's hand.

And when the shop closed, he still went up the hill to Carnegie's library.

He never stopped reading. Under the words "Let There Be Light," a young man in a grocer's coat sat at the long tables with the world spread open in front of him: travels and adventures, faraway places, and above all America. Every boy in Dunfermline knew the story of Andrew Carnegie, the weaver's son from this very town who had sailed for America with nothing and become the richest man in the world. Every time Laurence walked through those doors, he walked past the proof that a poor boy from Dunfermline could cross the ocean and become anything.

Meanwhile, Dunfermline was dying around him.

The war was over, and the town that had grown fat on it went hungry. In the middle of the decade the Admiralty shut the great dockyard at Rosyth down to care and maintenance, and the hammering that had gone on night and day when he was a boy fell silent. Thousands of men were thrown out of work. The linen mills were struggling. In the spring of 1926 came the General Strike, and then the long miners' lockout that closed the Fife pits all summer, and the streets were full of men standing on corners with their hands in their pockets and nothing to do. Every week, it seemed, someone else was leaving: a neighbor's son to Canada, a school friend to Australia, a whole family to America. The newspapers were full of advertisements for passages, and the Clyde was full of emigrant ships. In the 1920s some four hundred thousand people left Scotland, so many that the country actually shrank.

Laurence watched them go. Friends he had grown up with shook his hand on the street corner and said they were off to Detroit, to Toronto, to Chicago. Letters came back with photographs: young men in new suits standing beside motor cars, in cities where there was work for anyone who wanted it. He was a grocer's assistant in a dying town, with a widowed mother and a future that went no further than the end of Foundry Street.

He was a quiet young man. He did not say much. But something was growing in him that he could not put down.

Then, in the summer of 1926, Janet told them she was going.

Chapter 18. Janet Sails First

Dunfermline, July–September 1926

Janet went first. And Lizzie, who had once been the child left standing on the shore, became the mother waving goodbye.

The summer of 1926 was the worst Fife had known in a generation, and that summer Janet Douglas, twenty-six, sat down at the kitchen table on Foundry Street with news. She was going to be married. And then she was going to America.

His name was Thomas Crombie, a man from Kinross, thirty-two, steady and quiet, the kind of man a mother could trust with her daughter. On Friday, July 16, 1926, in Dunfermline, they were married. Picture the little party afterward in the flat: Lizzie in her best dress, laughing and wiping her eyes; Laurence, nineteen, tall and awkward in a borrowed collar, shaking his new brother-in-law's hand; the table loaded with what the family could afford, and for one evening nobody talking about the pits.

Then the tickets came.

America had shut its doors to most of the world two years before, but Britain's quota was still one of the largest, and a young Scottish couple with work in their hands and a little money in their pockets could still get in. Thousands of them were going the same way: by Canadian Pacific steamer to Canada, and by train across the border at Detroit. Within two months of the wedding, Janet and Thomas were ready to go.

Then the morning came. A trunk corded and labeled on the floor of the flat. Janet's coat on, her hat pinned. Lizzie fussing with a parcel of sandwiches for the journey, because there was nothing else her hands could do. And then the station, and the steam, and the guard's whistle, and Janet leaning out of the carriage window to hold her mother's hands one last time.

Think what that moment was for Lizzie.

Nearly fifty years before, a little girl of four had been left behind in Scotland while her mother sailed for the other side of the world and never came back. All her life Lizzie had carried that. She had built her whole life around never doing to anyone what had been done to her. She had married, kept a home, raised her daughter, and taken in another woman's son. And now here she was on a station platform, and it was her own child leaning out of a train, going across an ocean.

It was not the same. Janet was a grown woman going to a husband and a better life, with a mother's blessing, and she would write. But a heart does not know the difference. The train began to move. Lizzie walked beside it, then faster, still holding on, until she had to let go. Then she stood on the platform with her hand up and watched it out of sight.

Beside her, Laurence stood and watched too. Then he took his mother's arm and walked her home.

On September 13, 1926, Janet and Thomas Crombie crossed from Canada into the United States at Detroit, and went on to Chicago, to a little frame house on South Kostner Avenue, on the West Side. Thomas found work selling furniture. Janet's letters began to come: thin blue envelopes with American stamps, read aloud at the kitchen table on Foundry Street, and then read again, and then put away in a drawer and taken out and read a third time.

They told of a city like nothing in Scotland. Trains that ran overhead on iron bridges. Streets that went on for miles. Shops full of everything. Work. And Laurence, nineteen, a grocer's assistant in a dying town, read every one.

Now there were two of them at home: a widow of fifty-two and the son she had taken in. He went to work every morning and came home every night and put his wages in her hand. He was all she had left in Scotland. And she was all he had.

For three years, he stayed.

Part Five: The Crossing

Chapter 19. Last Days in Scotland

Dunfermline, spring–September 1929

In the spring of 1929 Janet came home. By the autumn, her brother had decided to leave with her.

She stepped off the Canadian Pacific liner Montroyal at Greenock on Saturday, May 18, 1929, alone, after nearly three years in America. Why she came, the family does not say. Perhaps to see her mother. Perhaps simply because she missed home. But it is not hard to picture what her coming did to the house.

Lizzie's life had changed too. She had taken a new name, Anderson, and a new home, at 39 Grieve Street in Dunfermline, and it was there that Janet came, with her American clothes and her American stories. That first night the kitchen must have been full: the kettle on, the good cups out, Lizzie unable to stop touching her daughter's hand, and Janet talking and talking. Chicago. The elevated trains roaring over the streets. Stores the size of whole villages. Electric light everywhere. Work for anybody who wanted it. And across the table, saying nothing, listening to every word, sat Laurence.

He was twenty-one. He had grown tall, about six feet, fair-haired and blue-eyed, with a long, fine-boned face. He was a grocer now, and a good one. He stood behind a long counter all day in a white apron, weighing out tea and sugar, slicing bacon, patting butter into shape with wooden pats, adding up bills in his head. It was respectable work. It was also the same work, in the same town, that he could see stretching out in front of him for the rest of his life.

There is a photograph of the two of them from the 1920s, its exact year and place unknown, and it is the best picture of him that survives.

They are standing against an iron railing in front of a whitewashed wall on a bright day, and both of them are eating ice-cream cones. Janet is small and neat in a long coat with big round buttons, her dark hair bobbed with a heavy fringe, a cone in each hand, one for herself and one waiting, and she is giving the camera a sidelong half-smile. Laurence towers over her. He leans back against the railing with one long leg crossed over the other, his hair combed straight back, his jacket pressed, a white handkerchief folded just so in his breast pocket, his eyes half closed as he bites into the ice cream. He looks young, and easy, and happy. In the family he was remembered as a man who was always impeccably dressed. The photograph agrees.

It is a picture of a brother and sister on a summer day. It may even be one of his last summers in Scotland.

Sometime that summer, he made up his mind.

No one wrote down the night he told Lizzie. But it is not hard to imagine. The supper things cleared away. The two of them at the kitchen table, Janet perhaps tactfully out of the room. Laurence turning his cup round and round in its saucer, trying to find a way to begin. And Lizzie, who knew him better than anyone in the world, looking at his face and knowing what he was going to say before he said it.

Think what it cost her to hear it. Robert was dead. Her daughter lived across an ocean. And now the boy she had taken in and raised as her own, the son who had put his wages in her hand every week since he was thirteen, was going too.

And think what it cost him to say it. He owed her everything. She was the only mother he had ever known. He was the one who had stayed. But he was twenty-two, and the town had nothing for him, and the whole of America was waiting on the other side of the sea.

She did not stop him. Of all the women in the world, Lizzie knew what it was to be left. And she let him go anyway, with her blessing, because she loved him, and because she wanted for him what every mother wants: more than she had had.

There was one more thing to do before he could sail.

To get into America, a man needed a visa, and to get a visa he had to show the consul his birth certificate. So Laurence sent to the register in Edinburgh for the paper that said who he was. And the paper that came back did not say Laurence Douglas.

It said Lawrence Brown.

Born June 14, 1907, at the Royal Maternity Hospital. Mother, Agnes Brown, domestic servant. Father: nothing. An empty space.

Did he know, before he held that paper in his hands, that he had been born with another name? No one can say. Perhaps Lizzie had told him long ago. Perhaps she sat down with him that summer and told him then, as gently as she could. Perhaps he had always half known. But here it was, in black and white, the name of a mother he could not remember and a father who did not exist on paper, and he could not get to America without it.

On Tuesday, September 3, 1929, at the American consulate in Glasgow, the United States issued an immigration visa. Not to Laurence Douglas, the boy from Foundry Street, the son of Robert and Lizzie. To Lawrence Brown.

To cross the ocean, he would have to become, once more, the baby his mother had named.

Four days later, he sailed.

Chapter 20. The Crossing

Greenock to Detroit, September 7–15, 1929

Fifty-one years after a little girl was left behind in Scotland while her mother sailed away, it was her turn to say goodbye to a ship.

Saturday, September 7, 1929. A great high-pressure system sat over the British Isles that day, and the forecast for the southwest of Scotland was "fair; warm." Down the Clyde estuary at Greenock, where Glasgow's emigrants had said goodbye to Scotland for a hundred years, the Canadian Pacific liner Duchess of Bedford lay at the quay with steam up, black hull, white upperworks, her two funnels smoking against the sky. She was a new ship, built just up the river at John Brown's yard at Clydebank, and she was bound for Canada.

Hundreds of them stood on the quay with trunks and bags and parcels tied with string. Porters shouting. Gulls wheeling and screaming over the water. Whole families in their best clothes, children clinging to their mothers' skirts, old men and women who had come to see sons and daughters off and knew in their hearts they would never see them again. Somewhere a piper, perhaps, because there often was. And the ship towering over all of it, the gangways sloping up into her side.

No one wrote down whether Lizzie came to Greenock that day. But if she could have come, she would have come. Picture her there in the crowd, a woman of fifty-five in her good coat and hat, holding the arm of a tall young man. Her daughter Janet, home for one summer, going back. And her son, the boy she had taken in, the boy who had been the whole reason her arms were not empty all those years, going with her.

Think what she must have seen, standing on that quay. Not just the ship. Not just her son's face. She would have seen herself, a little girl of four, in Scotland, while a ship carried her mother away to the far side of the world. She had lived her whole life with that day inside her. She had never once done to a child what had been done to her. And now, at fifty-five, she stood at the edge of the sea and let her boy go, because it was the best thing for him, and because loving someone sometimes means opening your hand.

The bell rang for visitors to go ashore.

Then the last embrace. Laurence bending down, a head taller than she was, to hold her. Lizzie's hand on the back of his neck, the way she had held him when he was small. Neither of them able to say any of what they meant. "Write to me." "I will. Every week." "Mind you eat." Small words, because the big ones would not come out. And then Janet taking his arm, and the two of them going up the gangway, and turning at the top to wave.

The lines were cast off. The tugs took the strain. Slowly, so slowly at first that it hardly seemed to move, the Duchess of Bedford drew away from the quay, and the gap of dark water between the ship and the shore grew wider, and wider, and the faces on the quay grew smaller. Handkerchiefs waved. People called out names. The ship's whistle let go a long, deep blast that echoed back off the hills.

At the rail, Laurence watched one small figure in a good coat and hat until he could no longer tell her from the crowd. And on the quay, Lizzie Douglas watched the ship until it was gone.

He had a ticket in his pocket for the cabin-class deck, a visa that called him Lawrence Brown, and fifty dollars in American money. And, the family says, a pocket watch, small and heavy, worn smooth at the edges, with one word inside its case. Edinburgh. The city where he had been born, under the name he was crossing the ocean to wear.

Down the estuary they steamed, past the Cloch lighthouse and the Cumbrae islands, past Arran's mountains standing up out of the sea, and out into the open Atlantic. That night the coast of Scotland sank into the dark behind him.

On the ship's list he wrote down who he was. Lawrence Brown, twenty-two, single, grocer, born in Edinburgh, last residence Dunfermline. And in the column for his nearest relative in the country he was leaving, he wrote: Mother, Mrs. Elizabeth Anderson, 39 Grieve Street. Whatever the paper in his pocket said, he knew who his mother was.

For six days the ship drove west across the Atlantic, and he could stand at the rail in the wind, watching the gray swells roll past, a young man with three thousand miles of water under him and the whole of his life ahead. Then icebergs, perhaps, far off and glittering, as the ship came round into the Strait of Belle Isle, and the high, dark coast of Labrador, and the mouth of the St. Lawrence, a river so wide you could not see across it.

On Friday, September 13, 1929, the Duchess came up the St. Lawrence to Quebec on a cold, wet day, and her passengers stepped down into the New World. There, before he went any farther, an American doctor of the Public Health Service looked him over and stamped his card: passed.

From there it was the train, west along the river and on through the long flat farmland of Ontario, until on Sunday, September 15, 1929, the train crossed the river from Windsor into Detroit, and stopped, and an American immigration inspector stepped aboard.

He filled out a card. It is the first description of Laurence that survives in the world.

Lawrence Brown. Twenty-two. Single. Grocer. Scottish. Born in Edinburgh. Bound for Chicago. Passage paid by himself. About six feet tall. Hair fair. Complexion fair. Eyes blue. Money in his possession: fifty dollars. Never before in the United States. Purpose in coming: seek work. Time he meant to stay: permanent.

The inspector stamped the card. The train pulled out across America.

Six feet. Fair hair. Blue eyes. Fifty dollars. Twenty-two years old, with his sister beside him and his whole life in front of him, rolling west toward Chicago in the last golden weeks of the greatest boom in American history.

Six weeks later, Wall Street crashed.

Part Six: Chicago

Chapter 21. Chicago

Chicago, Ill., 1929–1936

He stepped off the train into the biggest, loudest, fastest city he had ever imagined.

Chicago in the autumn of 1929 was a city of three and a third million people, and it roared. Elevated trains thundered overhead on iron trestles, so close to the windows of the flats that you could see people eating their suppers as you rattled past. Streetcars clanged down the avenues. Downtown, in the Loop, the skyscrapers stood in canyons of stone and the sidewalks were packed shoulder to shoulder from morning until night. Motor cars, more than he had seen in his whole life, honked and jostled at every corner. To a young man from the quiet gray streets of Dunfermline it must have felt like stepping into the middle of a brass band.

He went to his sister's house on South Kostner Avenue, on the West Side, in a neighborhood called North Lawndale: block after block of brick two-flats and little frame cottages, with porches and back stairs and clotheslines strung between them, alive with the sound of Yiddish and Czech and English and a dozen other languages. Janet and Thomas took him in as a lodger. He had a bed, a place at their table, and a city full of work.

For six weeks, America was everything Janet's letters had promised.

Then, at the end of October 1929, the stock market broke. Black Thursday. Black Tuesday. Billions of dollars vanished in a few days, and the panic went out from Wall Street across the whole country like a crack running through ice. At first it seemed like something that happened only to rich men in New York. Then the factories began to lay men off. Then the banks began to close their doors. And the boom that had brought him across the ocean was over almost before he had unpacked.

He kept working. In the spring of 1930, when the census taker came to the house on Kostner Avenue, he found him there, at the top of the second page, listed as Thomas Crombie's brother-in-law: Lawrence Brown, twenty-two, a salesman in a grocery. He had brought his trade across the ocean with him, and in the worst of times a grocer was one of the last men to be let go, because whatever else people stopped buying, they still had to eat.

On paper he was still Lawrence Brown.

Then the hard times got harder.

By the winter of 1932 and 1933, more than a third of the working people of Chicago had no job at all. Men who had been foremen and clerks and craftsmen stood in breadlines that wound around whole city blocks, in thin coats, with their collars turned up against the wind off Lake Michigan. Families were put out on the sidewalk with their furniture for not paying the rent. The city ran so short of money that for months it paid its schoolteachers in paper scrip, or not at all. Shacktowns grew up in the empty lots, and men rode the freight trains looking for work that did not exist.

Through all of it, Laurence held on. He went to work every morning behind a grocery counter and came home every night, and he put his money where it was needed, and he did not go under. By 1934 he was living a few doors along Kostner Avenue, at number 1518. He was still the impeccably dressed young man of the ice-cream photograph, his suit pressed, his hair combed, even in the years when so many around him had lost everything. It was a kind of pride, and a kind of courage: whatever the times were doing, he would not let them make him look beaten.

On October 3, 1936, Janet gave birth to twin daughters, Elizabeth, called Betty, and Jessie, and Laurence became an uncle at twenty-nine. Nearly thirty years before, he had been a baby with no father's name on his paper. Now there was family around him.

And by then, or soon after, he had made up his mind to go.

Somewhere along the way, behind the grocery counter, he had found something he was better at than weighing out sugar. A grocer has to make his shelves and his window look tempting, and Laurence had an eye. He knew how to make a pyramid of tins look like a cathedral, how to set out a window so that people stopped on the sidewalk to look. It was a gift, and a gift like that could be a trade.

Sometime between the spring of 1935 and the end of 1936, he packed his good suits, said goodbye to his sister, and went east, to a small, old city on a bay in New England, where he did not know a soul.

When he got there, he left Lawrence Brown behind, and became Laurence Douglas again.

Part Seven: The Long Road

Chapter 22. Weybosset Street

Providence, R.I., 1937–1938

It is late at night on Weybosset Street, and the only light on the whole block is in one shop window.

Inside it, a tall young man in his shirtsleeves is on his knees with a mouthful of pins. Around him stand the mannequins, stiff and patient in their new fall suits. He reaches up and tugs a lapel straight. He steps out onto the dark sidewalk, studies the window with his head on one side, goes back in, and moves a hat two inches to the left. Out again. Better. He tilts a tie on its stand, sets a pair of gloves just so on the edge of a chair, angles a lamp until the light falls exactly where he wants it. Then he stands back on the empty sidewalk, alone in the cold, and looks at what he has made. In the morning, a thousand people will walk past it on their way to work, and some of them will stop.

By 1937 Laurence Douglas had found his art.

Providence, Rhode Island, was an old, compact city of brick and granite at the head of Narragansett Bay, built on the mills and jewelry shops of New England, and in the 1930s its downtown was alive. Westminster Street and Weybosset Street ran side by side, a block apart, lined with department stores and specialty shops, banks, theaters and lunch counters, and every Saturday the crowds came in on the streetcars from the mill neighborhoods to shop, or, nine years into the Depression, simply to look. At the heart of it stood the Outlet Company, the city's great department store, filling most of a block.

Directly across Weybosset Street from the Outlet, in a handsome six-story brick building at number 165, Laurence was display manager. He had arrived in a city where he knew no one, with nothing but a good suit, a good eye and a name he had taken back, and within a year or two he was in charge of the windows of a store on one of the best shopping streets in New England.

In those days the shop window was everything. There was no television. Most people did not have a car to drive out to a shopping plaza, because there were no shopping plazas. You came downtown and you walked, and the windows were how a store called out to you. A man walking down Weybosset Street could pass fifty of them in ten minutes: mannequins in fall coats against painted autumn woods, a pyramid of new radios, a bride and her attendants in white, and at Christmas, windows with moving figures and falling snow that drew crowds three deep, children with their noses against the glass. The men who made those windows were called display men, or window trimmers, and the best of them were paid like artists, because they were.

Laurence was one of the best. He had always cared how things looked. In the family, the thing everyone remembered about him was that he was never anything less than impeccably dressed: a good suit, carefully pressed, a white shirt, the tie knotted just so, polished shoes, his hair combed perfectly into place. It was the look of the man and the look of his trade. A display man was in the business of making things look right, and he lived it.

He lived simply. He took rented rooms, first on Elmwood Avenue, then on Pine Street, a short walk from the store. He still loved to read, and the Providence Public Library, in its marble building at Empire and Washington Streets, was only a few minutes from Weybosset Street: a great public library again, for a man who had grown up in Carnegie's.

He was thirty. He had a trade, a name, a city. What he did not have was anyone to come home to.

And one block north, on Westminster Street, up a flight of stairs in a building full of offices and shops, a young woman with a pleasant smile was setting another woman's hair.

Her workplace and his were a few minutes' walk apart.

Her name was Constance Eugenie Darcy.

Chapter 23. Constance

Providence, R.I., 1888–1938

She was the kind of person who made a room feel warmer just by walking into it.

Constance Eugenie Darcy was born in Providence on Saturday, October 12, 1912, in a house on Smith Street, the street that climbs up past the white marble dome of the State House. She came from working people on both sides. Her father, Albert Darcy, from a family of Irish descent, was a printer, a man who set type on the great clattering Linotype machines of the newspapers, tall, with light blue eyes and dark brown hair. Her mother, Eugenie, had been born across the border in Sutton, Quebec, one of the many thousands of French Canadians whose families had come down from Quebec to find work in the mill towns of New England. They married young: he was twenty-three, she was seventeen. Constance was their first child.

Her childhood moved where her father's work moved: Boston for a while, then Taunton, Massachusetts, where her little brother, Albert Norman, was born when she was three, then back to Providence and East Providence, from one rented flat to another, wherever the newspapers needed a man at the keyboard.

In time her parents went their separate ways. Her mother made a home of her own, working as a decorator, and Constance grew up between the two households, close to her mother. It was not an easy thing for a girl in those years. But it did not make her hard. If anything, it made her kind.

She was pleasant, congenial and easy to be with, the kind of woman who put everyone around her at ease. In the family, that is what everyone remembers about her first. And she found the perfect work for it.

In the 1930s the beauty parlor was a little world of its own. Women came in tired and went out feeling like somebody: hair washed and set, marcelled into waves, permed under the great domed driers that hummed along the wall, while the radio played and everybody talked. Constance trained as a hairdresser, and by the mid-1930s she was at work downtown, on Westminster Street, the busiest shopping street in Rhode Island, in a salon up a flight of stairs. All day long women sat in her chair and told her their troubles, and she listened, and laughed, and made them look lovely, and sent them back out into the Depression feeling a little better than when they came in.

Years later she would bring that same gift to the bedsides of the sick, as a nurse.

By 1938 she was twenty-five, and she and her mother were sharing a flat on Dartmouth Avenue. Every working day she went downtown to Westminster Street. And one block away, on Weybosset Street, a tall, quiet Scotsman in a beautifully pressed suit was dressing the windows she walked past.

How they met, no one wrote down.

Perhaps she stopped one evening in front of his window, and he was inside it, on his knees with a mouthful of pins, and he looked up. Perhaps they ate their lunch at the same counter. Perhaps a friend introduced them. However it happened, it is not hard to see what each of them found. He was handsome and quiet and immaculate, a man of few words with a soft Scottish voice, a man who loved books and made beautiful things. She was warm and easy and full of life, the kind of woman who could make a quiet man talk and a lonely man laugh.

He had grown up without his mother. She had grown up between two homes. Each of them, in their own way, had been looking for one.

Neither of them knew that in the autumn of 1938, the sea itself was coming for the streets where they worked.

Chapter 24. The Hurricane of 1938

Providence, R.I., September 21, 1938

Nobody told them it was coming.

Wednesday, September 21, 1938, began in Providence like any other working day. It had rained for four days and the rivers were high, but the morning came up bright, and the streetcars rolled downtown full as usual. On Weybosset Street, across from the Outlet, was the store where Laurence Douglas dressed the windows. One block north, up a flight of stairs on Westminster Street, was where Constance Darcy worked. Where each of them was that morning, no one recorded. The radios of Providence said nothing about a storm.

Down in the Atlantic, the most powerful hurricane in New England's memory was racing north at fifty miles an hour.

The forecasters in Washington had tracked it up the coast for days, and they were sure it would curve harmlessly out to sea. It did not. It shot north like a bullet, crossed Long Island in the afternoon, and came ashore on the Connecticut coast at the worst possible hour: the time of the equinox, close to the top of the highest tide of the month. And downtown Providence stood at the very end of Narragansett Bay, at the narrow tip of a funnel nearly thirty miles long, with the whole weight of the sea about to be poured into it.

By midday the clouds were racing low over the rooftops. By two the wind was rising in long, rising howls, and people on the street held on to their hats and laughed about it. By three o'clock no one was laughing.

People who heard that wind said it sounded like a freight train that never passed. Signs tore loose and went sailing down the street. Chimneys came down. And then the plate glass began to go. All over the business district there was a sound like gunfire, a steady popping and crashing, the sound of the great shop windows of Providence bursting out of their frames one after another. On one street the entire front window of a furniture store came out in a single sheet and was carried up the street on the wind, turning over and over, until it exploded on the sidewalk.

Picture Laurence in that moment. The windows were his whole life, his art, the thing he was proudest of. And all around him, up and down the street, they were shattering.

And picture where his mind went next. If he had already met her by then, and with their workplaces a block apart he may well have, then there was a girl in a beauty parlor one block north, on the other street the storm was about to hit.

Late in the afternoon, the sea came.

Along the shore of Rhode Island, at Westerly and Watch Hill and Misquamicut and Narragansett, the storm surge rose like a wall and swept whole rows of summer cottages into the ocean with people still inside them. Then the water came up the bay. The gale held the high tide in and drove more and more water north, past Newport, past Prudence Island, where the lighthouse keeper's wife and son drowned, past Warwick, into the throat of the Providence River, and straight into the heart of the city.

It arrived at about five o'clock, just as the offices and stores were letting out and thousands of people were spilling onto the streets to go home.

It came up first through the drains, bubbling out of the gratings. Then the river came over its banks and poured into the business district. In Exchange Place, in front of City Hall, water that had been ankle deep was waist deep within minutes, then chest deep. It ran up Weybosset Street and through the doors of the shops. Parked cars floated and were carried away toward the river. Streetcars stood abandoned with water to their windows. And as the sea reached the wiring of the drowned cars, their horns began to sound, all of them at once, and went on sounding, a long, eerie, endless wail under the howl of the wind. People who lived through it said that was the strangest sound of the whole day: a whole city of cars crying out under the water.

Into the streets came the wreckage of the bay: boats torn from their moorings, oil drums, timbers, the drowned stock of the downtown stores. At about five o'clock the wind gauge at the Weather Bureau recorded eighty-seven miles an hour and then blew away. People trapped outdoors climbed anything that stood above the water. They clung to lampposts. They crowded the steps of City Hall. At the Biltmore Hotel, staff threw bedsheets down from the second floor and hauled people out of the flood. In the office buildings, those who had not yet left climbed to the upper floors and stood at the windows, watching the water fill the floors beneath them. Some of the people who stayed in their cars drowned in them.

No one wrote down where Laurence and Constance were in that hour. But their workplaces were downtown, a block apart, on the two streets the water reached. Perhaps he fought his way north through the wind toward Westminster Street, a tall man bent double, one arm over his face against the flying grit and glass. Perhaps she stood up her flight of stairs at a rattling window, looking down at a street that had turned into a river, and wondering where he was.

When the water crested, it stood thirteen feet eight and a half inches above mean high water, higher than the great flood of 1815, the worst the city had ever known. Today a bronze marker on the old Market House shows the line it reached. It is far above a tall man's head.

Then, as quickly as it had come, it was over. By half past five the wind was dropping. The storm was already racing on north over Massachusetts toward Vermont. In the dark the water began to drain back toward the bay almost as fast as it had come in. Downtown Providence was black: no lights, no power, nothing but the sound of water running and glass crunching underfoot. Looters came out while the water was still high, and the National Guard was sent in to patrol the streets.

In the gray morning, the city came back to see what was left. Downtown was a ruin of drowned cars, broken boats, smashed glass and ruined merchandise. A steamer lay sunk at Fox Point. Every shop on Westminster and Weybosset Streets had been flooded. In Rhode Island almost four hundred people were dead, most of them along the shore. Across New England the storm killed more than five hundred and sixty.

Laurence and Constance were not among them.

The stores swept out the mud, and the glaziers came, and the windows went back in, and Laurence went back to dressing them. But some things look different after a night like that. When you have seen the sea come up a city street and take people away, you know how fast everything can be gone. You know not to wait.

Soon the couple welcomed their first child, a daughter, Eugenie.

Chapter 25. A Wedding in Alabama

Birmingham, Ala., October 9, 1939

They were married a thousand miles from home, in a city where neither of them knew a soul, a month after the world went to war.

Why Birmingham, Alabama? No one in the family remembers. Perhaps there was work there, or a friend, or a reason only the two of them ever knew. Perhaps they simply wanted to begin their married life somewhere new, just the two of them, away from everyone. Whatever the reason, in the first days of October 1939, Laurence Douglas and Constance Darcy were in the steel city of the South.

It was a strange, bright, frightening autumn. On the first of September, Germany had invaded Poland. On the third, Britain declared war, and that same evening, northwest of Ireland, a German submarine torpedoed the liner Athenia, outward bound from Glasgow to Montreal, the same crossing Laurence had made ten years before. More than a hundred people died in the dark water. Back in Scotland, the young men of Dunfermline were being called up.

America was not at war. Not yet. In Birmingham, the sun was hot and the leaves had not begun to turn.

On Monday, October 9, 1939, they went to the Jefferson County Courthouse. It was a new building then, tall and white and modern, in the clean, soaring style of the day, with carved figures over its doors and painted murals in its halls. Picture them climbing its wide steps in the heat, which reached eighty-eight degrees that afternoon: Laurence, thirty-two, tall and immaculate in his best suit, a fresh handkerchief folded in his breast pocket; Constance, twenty-six, three days short of her twenty-seventh birthday, in her best dress, her hair, of course, perfect.

There was no church, no organ, no family. No mother to cry in the front pew. No sister to fuss with her veil. Perhaps no one at all who had known them for long. Just a clerk's office in the probate judge's wing, and a ledger, and a pen.

The clerk asked his questions and wrote the answers down.

The groom: Lawrence Douglas, thirty-two, born in Edinburgh, Scotland. A salesman. And when the clerk asked the names of his parents, Laurence did not hesitate. Robert Douglas. Elizabeth McDonald. The forester's son who had walked him to school, and the girl from Gilmerton who had been left behind and grown up to take him in. Not the name on his birth certificate. The people who had raised him.

The bride: Constance Eugenie Darcy, twenty-six, born in Providence. Her parents, Albert B. Darcy and Eugenie Delaire.

A judge of the city court, Thomas R. Lea, read the words. They made their promises. He put the ring on her finger. In a strange courthouse, far from anyone who had ever known him as a boy, a clerk wrote down a new beginning for Laurence. Every other name and paper in his life had been handed to him. This one he had chosen.

The next day, notices with their names appeared in two Birmingham newspapers, two strangers in a city that would not remember them.

But they would remember. They were married for twenty-one years, through a world war, through moves from one end of the country to the other, through good times and hard ones, until the day in March 1961 when he died. Her name and his are on the same stone today.

The world was going to war. They were going west.

Chapter 26. San Francisco

San Francisco, Calif., 1940–1941

For a little while, at the far edge of the continent, they had everything.

By the spring of 1940, Laurence and Constance had crossed the whole of America and come to rest in San Francisco, in a flat on Valencia Street in the Mission District. It was as far from Dunfermline as a man could go without falling into the Pacific.

San Francisco in 1940 was the most beautiful city in America, and it knew it. The Golden Gate Bridge had opened three years before, and it hung across the mouth of the bay like a great red harp, with the fog pouring in under it in the afternoons like milk. Out on Treasure Island the Golden Gate International Exposition was running its second summer: palaces and fountains and towers lit up at night in colors that shimmered across the water. Cable cars clanged up the impossible hills. The whole city looked out over the bay as if it had just been built for the view.

The Mission was the sunny side of town, out of the worst of the fog, a working neighborhood of Irish and German and Italian families, of wooden flats with bay windows painted in pale colors, church bells on Sunday mornings, and corner stores with oranges and lemons piled in the window. Their flat would have been full of light from the bay window, with the radio on and the smell of the sea coming in on the wind.

And downtown, at the corner of Post and Kearny, two blocks from Union Square, stood the store of Bond Clothes, one of the biggest men's clothing chains in America. Its windows were Laurence's.

He was the window trimmer, the display man, the artist of the corner. Every few weeks the windows changed: suits for spring, topcoats for winter, white dinner jackets for the summer dances, ties laid out like a fan of playing cards. He worked forty-eight hours a week and earned about forty-three dollars, a good wage in 1940 for a man who had arrived in America eleven years before with fifty.

A grocer's message boy from Foundry Street, dressing the windows of one of the great shopping corners of San Francisco. It was the American story Carnegie's library had promised him.

They moved round the corner to a bigger flat on Julian Avenue, a four-story building on a short, quiet street. In the evenings they could walk to the top of the hill and watch the lights come on across the city.

The war was still somebody else's war. In Europe it was terrible: France had fallen, and London was burning in the Blitz, and in Scotland the old dockyard at Rosyth was alive again with warships. But America was at peace, and it meant to stay that way. Still, in October 1940, the government called on every man between twenty-one and thirty-five to register for a possible draft. Laurence went down to the local board on Valencia Street with sixteen million other men across the country, and signed his card. For the person who would always know where he was, he wrote one name: his wife, Constance.

In June 1941 the two of them went north, up the coast into Canada, for reasons nobody wrote down: a holiday, perhaps, or the nearest thing a working couple in those years ever had to a honeymoon. Coming back across the border at Blaine, Washington, on a bus, on Saturday, June 21, 1941, the inspector looked at the tall Scotsman and wrote down what he saw: Lawrence Brown Douglas, displayman, about six feet, brown hair, blue eyes, returning to his home in San Francisco with his wife, an American citizen. His hair had darkened since the boy at Detroit. He had turned thirty-four the week before.

It was the last summer of peace.

The next day, Germany invaded Russia. Six months later, a Sunday morning in Hawaii would end the world they knew.

Chapter 27. The Night of the Sirens

San Francisco, Calif., December 1941

San Francisco Chronicle

Japan Planes Near S.F. — 4 Raid Alarms

Tuesday, December 9, 1941

Twenty-five years after the zeppelins came over Edinburgh, the war came looking for his family again. This time he was three thousand miles away, and his wife faced it alone.

In the autumn of 1941, the family was divided. Laurence had gone east to work in the Pittsburgh district, as the family remembers it, while Constance stayed behind in San Francisco. Why they were apart, no one wrote down. Perhaps there was a better job waiting in the East and he went ahead to make a home for them. Whatever the reason, in December 1941 the whole width of America lay between them, and the only thing that crossed it was the mail and, for those who could afford it, the long-distance telephone.

Sunday, December 7, began like any Sunday in San Francisco. The churches of the Mission were full for the late Masses. Families strolled in Golden Gate Park. In the flat, perhaps, the breakfast things were cleared away, the radio was murmuring in the corner, and a letter from Pittsburgh stood propped against the clock.

At eleven-thirty the music on the radio stopped.

A voice came on, fast and strange. Japanese planes had attacked Pearl Harbor, in Hawaii. The Pacific Fleet had been bombed at anchor. There were many dead. All over the city people came out of their houses and stood in the street asking each other whether it was true. All through the afternoon the bulletins came, broken and contradictory, each one worse than the last. Ships sunk. Airfields burning. Nobody knew how bad it was. Nobody knew what was coming next.

And everybody in San Francisco knew one thing. Hawaii was two thousand miles out in the Pacific. San Francisco was the next great city on the coast. If the Japanese could reach Pearl Harbor, they could reach here.

By that afternoon soldiers were guarding the Golden Gate Bridge and the tunnels. Volunteers poured into the police stations. The mayor declared an emergency.

Then, on Monday night, a little after six o'clock, it began.

The Army reported enemy planes, thirty or more, ranging over the bay from San Jose to the navy yard at Mare Island. The order went out: black out the city. All of it, now. And San Francisco, which had never in its history prepared for an air raid, did not know how. There were almost no sirens: one on the Ferry Building, a few on the bridges. So the police cars and fire engines tore through the streets with their own sirens screaming, and volunteer wardens ran from door to door, pounding and shouting up at the windows: "Turn out your lights! Turn out your lights!" Down on Market Street the great neon signs were on time switches that nobody could turn off, and they went on blazing red and green and gold into the dark, like a target painted on the city.

Picture Constance in that hour.

The lights out. The flat pitch black. The sirens wailing up and down the streets outside, rising and falling, and the wardens shouting below, and somewhere a dog barking. Constance feeling her way across a room she suddenly could not see, and sitting there in the blackness, listening. Listening for the planes. People in the streets said afterward that they heard the drone of engines overhead, and gunfire out over the ocean.

And the person she wanted most in the world was not there. He was on the other side of the continent, in a city where it was a perfectly ordinary Monday night. There was no way to reach him. No way to tell him she was all right. No way to know whether, by morning, there would be a city left to be all right in.

She was twenty-nine years old. She held on, and she waited.

The sirens went four times that night. Each time the city went dark and held its breath, and each time, after an hour or so, the all-clear came, and the lights crept back on, and people looked at each other and began to breathe again. On Tuesday morning the Chronicle's front page shouted it across the top: Japan Planes Near S.F. — 4 Raid Alarms.

No bomb ever fell on San Francisco. The planes, if there were planes, were never found.

But nobody who sat in the dark that night forgot it. Constance knew now what her husband had known since he was eight years old, standing at a window in Dunfermline with a red sky over the Forth: the world could come for you in the dark, out of nowhere, and all you could do was hold on to the ones you loved and wait.

It was time for the family to be together.

Chapter 28. An American Citizen

Pittsburgh and Avalon, Pa., 1941–1943

He raised his right hand in a federal courtroom in Pittsburgh, and with a few sentences he stopped being a subject of the King and became an American, under both of his names.

By 1943 the family was together again, in Pennsylvania. Pittsburgh at war was the arsenal of democracy. Its steel mills ran day and night along the Monongahela and the Ohio, the furnaces lighting the sky orange after dark, the smoke so thick that on winter days the streetlights burned at noon. Laurence was display manager for Bond Stores in the city now, in charge of the windows, and the family lived a few miles down the Ohio, in a quiet hillside borough called Avalon, in rooms over a brick building on California Avenue.

It was a home-front life, the same as every family's. Victory gardens in the back lots. Tin cans and kitchen fat saved for the war effort. Ration books for sugar and coffee and gasoline. Blue-star flags in the front windows of houses with sons in the service, and here and there, as the war went on, a gold star, which meant a son who would not come home. Even the suits in Laurence's windows went to war: to save cloth, the government banned trouser cuffs and the two-trouser suit that Bond had built its whole trade on.

And once again, as when he was a boy in Dunfermline, the enemy was Germany.

He had been in America for thirteen years. He had married an American. He dressed the windows of an American store and paid American taxes and read American newspapers about American boys fighting in North Africa. But on paper he was still a foreigner, a British subject, born in Edinburgh. It was time to make it right.

On February 11, 1943, nine days after the last German army at Stalingrad surrendered, he went to the federal courthouse in Pittsburgh and filed his petition to become a citizen of the United States.

The petition asked him who he was, and the answer took up both of his lives.

Laurence Brown Douglas. Display manager. Born in Edinburgh, Scotland, June 14, 1907. Five feet eleven inches tall, one hundred and fifty-two pounds. Complexion fair. Eyes blue. Hair brown. Arrived at Detroit on September 15, 1929, under the name Lawrence Brown. Wife, Constance, born in Providence.

Two Pittsburgh men, a machinist and a clerk, signed as his witnesses and swore that he was a man of good character. From San Francisco came sworn statements from two friends who had known the family there. The government checked his papers, his years, his story.

Then, on Thursday, May 6, 1943, he came back to the courthouse to take the oath.

Picture the courtroom. High windows, dark wood, the American flag beside the judge's bench. A row of men and women from every country in the world, Poles and Italians and Greeks and Irish and Germans, some in their best suits, some in the clothes they had come straight from the mill in, all of them nervous, all of them dressed as well as they could manage. Wives and children in the seats behind. And among them, impeccable as always, his suit pressed and his hair combed perfectly into place, a tall Scotsman of thirty-five.

The clerk read out the oath, and they repeated it after him.

I hereby declare, on oath, that I absolutely and entirely renounce and abjure all allegiance and fidelity to any foreign prince, potentate, state or sovereignty, and particularly to the King of Great Britain.

Think what those words meant to him. The King of Great Britain. The country of the cottages under Tinto and the apartment blocks of Edinburgh, of Foundry Street and Carnegie's library, of the battlecruisers in the Forth and the red sky over Leith. The country where Robert Douglas was buried, and where Lizzie had let him go. He was not giving up any of that. He would carry all of it with him. But he was choosing, out loud, in front of a judge, where his life belonged.

That I will support and defend the Constitution and laws of the United States of America against all enemies, foreign and domestic.

The judge granted the petition. And the boy who had been registered in Edinburgh in 1907 as Lawrence Brown, son of Agnes, who had grown up as Laurence Douglas, son of Robert and Lizzie, who had crossed the ocean as Lawrence Brown and come back to Douglas in Providence, walked out of that courtroom into the spring sunshine as Laurence Brown Douglas, a citizen of the United States.

Both names at once. The mother who had borne him and the family that had raised him, side by side, in a single line of type.

A week later, the last German army in North Africa surrendered in Tunisia. The tide of the war had turned. And when it was over, Laurence would take his family home.

Part Eight: Rhode Island

Chapter 29. River Avenue

Providence and Pawtucket, R.I., 1945–1960

After all the miles and all the names, he came home to stay.

When the war ended, Laurence brought his family back to Rhode Island, where Constance had grown up and where he had first dressed windows on Weybosset Street. He was nearly forty now: a citizen, a husband, a father, a man with a trade in his hands. He had lived in Edinburgh and Dunfermline, Chicago and Providence, Birmingham and San Francisco and Pittsburgh. He had crossed an ocean and a continent and back again. He was done with moving far.

They settled first in Pawtucket, the old mill city on the falls of the Blackstone River just north of Providence, where Samuel Slater had built America's first successful cotton mill in 1793. For a century and a half the city had lived by textiles: thread and lace and yarn and cloth spun in great brick mills along the river. The family took a flat on Magill Street, in the Woodlawn section, a street of wooden three-deckers with porches stacked one on top of another and clotheslines strung across the back yards, full of French Canadian and Irish, Portuguese and Polish families. Constance's people. Their kind of street.

Laurence went to work for the stores of Pawtucket, dressing their windows along Main Street. For the next fifteen years, the merchants of that mill city would hire him to make their windows shine.

He and Constance raised three daughters, Eugenie, Constance and Norma. In the evening, a tall man came up the porch steps in a perfectly pressed suit, home from work. After supper, his chair, his lamp, his book. He still loved to read, the way he had loved it as a boy in Carnegie's library, and he still went nowhere without looking his best. The family remembers him always immaculate, his hair combed perfectly into place.

He had lost the father who raised him at twelve and left his mother and his country at twenty-two. Now, at last, he had a home of his own, and he was the one at the head of the table.

By 1950 they had moved into Providence, to General Street, in the North End, near the Pawtucket line. And in 1956 they moved one last time, a short way across the North End, to 644 River Avenue.

River Avenue ran through Elmhurst, a neighborhood of wooden two- and three-family houses, mostly Irish and Italian, Catholic and working: fathers in the shops and the post office and the fire department, mothers raising big families in six-room flats, children everywhere. The parish was St. Pius V. Farther along the avenue stood Providence College, and the Dominican friars in their long white habits were a familiar sight on the sidewalks. It was a good street, an ordinary American street, and for a boy from Foundry Street it was everything.

It was the last house he would ever live in.

The years brought their storms and their sorrows too. In the summer of 1954 Hurricane Carol came roaring up Narragansett Bay and flooded downtown Providence for the second time in sixteen years, and the sea came into the streets where Laurence had first dressed windows, all over again. That October, Constance's brother, Albert, died, only thirty-eight, and was buried at Saint Ann Cemetery in Cranston. Her father, the old Linotype man, followed in 1957. In Chicago, Janet and Thomas had both become American citizens, the whole little family from Foundry Street American now.

And around them, slowly, Rhode Island was changing. One by one the great textile mills that had built the state were closing their doors and moving south, where labor was cheaper. Every year there were fewer mill families with money in their pockets on a Saturday, and fewer shoppers on Main Street, and fewer windows to dress. In 1960 he was the displayman for Carter's Men Store in Pawtucket. By the next year he had gone into business for himself, Laurence Douglas, window displays, working out of the house on River Avenue.

Constance went back to work too. She trained at the Rhode Island School of Practical Nursing, and by 1961 she was a nurse at Roger Williams General Hospital, a few minutes' drive from home. The gift that had put tired women at ease in her beauty-parlor chair now comforted the sick in their hospital beds.

In the winter of 1960 there was a house on River Avenue with the lights on and snow on the porch rail. A husband and wife both working, both tired, both proud of what they had built. Laurence was fifty-three. He had come so far from the cottage under the cherry trees, from the message boy on Foundry Street, from the young man with fifty dollars at Detroit. He had made a life.

Nobody in that house knew that it was his last winter.

Chapter 30. The Ninth of March

Providence, R.I., March 1961

It came out of nowhere, the way the worst things in his life always had.

In the winter of 1961 he was fifty-three years old, a tall, quiet man in a pressed suit who went out every morning with his tools and his pins and his good eye to dress other people's windows. He was not an old man. He had a business of his own now, a wife who loved him, a family, and a house on River Avenue with snow on the porch rail. There was every reason to think there were years ahead.

Then he was ill. And then, suddenly, he was in the hospital.

Nobody at home had known. That is what the family remembered afterward, more than anything: how little warning there was. One week he was going to work. The next he was lying in a bed at St. Joseph's Hospital in South Providence, and the doctors were saying words that no one wanted to hear. The family understood it to be lung cancer, and that it had been growing in him, silent, for longer than anyone could have guessed. By the time anyone knew, there was nothing anyone could do.

Picture Constance in those days. She was a nurse. She knew what the doctors' faces meant before they said a word. She had sat beside other people's husbands and fathers in beds like his and held other women's hands. Now it was her own husband, and all her training, all her gentleness, all the gift she had for putting frightened people at ease, came down to sitting beside him, holding his hand, and trying not to let him see how afraid she was.

Thursday, March 9, 1961. A storm came up the coast in the dark, and all that day Providence had fog, and snow, and sleet, and rain, and the streets and trees and wires began to glaze over with ice. The city went slow and quiet and gray.

That day, at St. Joseph's Hospital, Laurence Brown Douglas died.

He was fifty-three years, eight months and twenty-three days old.

He had been born in a maternity hospital in Edinburgh to a young woman with no one beside her. He had been given into the arms of a woman who had once been left behind herself. He had walked to school with a forester's son, grown up beside a war that set his birthplace on fire, lost the father who raised him at twelve, carried groceries up the steep streets of Dunfermline, and stood at the rail of a ship and watched Scotland disappear. He had come through the Depression on a grocer's wage and become an artist of windows. He had survived the sea in the streets of Providence, married the girl a block away, crossed America twice, sworn himself a citizen in the middle of a war, and raised a family in a house that was his own.

And now, on a gray, icy day in March, in a hospital bed in Providence, it was over.

On Saturday, the Pawtucket Times printed his obituary on page three. It said he had been born in Edinburgh, Scotland. It said he was the son of the late Robert and Elizabeth (Anderson) Douglas. It said he had worked for the Bond Clothing Company, and had his own display business, and that for the past fifteen years he had dressed windows for Pawtucket firms. It named his wife, Constance, his family, and his sister, Mrs. Thomas Crombie of Chicago.

It did not name Agnes Brown.

The newspaper gave the facts of a working life. The family remembered other things. His friends called him Doug. He was always impeccably dressed, his hair perfectly combed. He was quiet. He loved to read.

On Monday, March 13, they buried him at Saint Ann Cemetery in Cranston, in the family lot where Constance's young brother, Albert, already lay.

Later they raised a stone.

LAURENCE B. DOUGLAS
1907–1961

Look closely at it. Between his first name and the name of the family that raised him, the stone keeps a single letter: B.

B, for Brown.

The name his mother gave him in an office on Forrest Road in 1907, on the morning she signed the register alone. The name that stayed with him, as an initial, when the Douglases took him in. The name that went with him to school, across the ocean and into the courtroom in Pittsburgh. Fifty-three years later, after everything, it was still with him, cut into granite in a cemetery in Rhode Island, a single letter holding a whole lost life.

Thirty-three years later, the same stone would carry Constance's name too.

Epilogue. The Names He Carried

Rhode Island, 1961–1994

He was gone. But the women who had carried him were not finished with his story.

Constance was forty-eight when her husband died, a widow with a house full of his things. She did what she had always done. She kept going. She kept nursing, at the Briarcliffe Health Center in Johnston and then for eight years at Cranston General Hospital, sitting beside the sick and the frightened and putting them at ease, until she retired in 1974. Her mother, Eugenie, died in 1966 and was buried at Saint Ann, not far from Laurence.

In her last years Constance lived in East Greenwich, on Sixth Street, and went to Mass at St. Francis de Sales. She was the same as she had always been: pleasant, warm, easy to be with, the kind of woman people were glad to sit beside. She died at Rhode Island Hospital on Tuesday, April 12, 1994, at eighty-one. They buried her beside him, in lot 84 at Saint Ann, thirty-three years after him, and cut her name into the stone next to his.

Janet, the big sister who had walked him up the hill to school and taken him across the ocean, lived out her life in Chicago with Thomas and their twin girls. She died in December 1979, at seventy-nine.

Of Lizzie, the woman who had been left behind at four and grew up to take him in, the records keep only a little more: a new name, Anderson, and a house on Grieve Street in Dunfermline, the address he gave for his mother the day he sailed. By the time he died, his obituary called her "the late Elizabeth." She had let him go so that he could have a life. He had. Every time he signed his name, Douglas, he carried hers.

And Agnes.

Agnes McGarth Brown, who let him go so that he could have more than she could give, married Thomas McKenzie in 1911 and raised two daughters, Mary and Jean. One portrait of her is known: a head-and-shoulders picture of a woman with a steady, serious face. Whether she ever learned what became of the boy she gave away, whether she ever knew that he crossed the ocean, made a life and married, no record says. But she had given him the only things she had to give: his life, and his name. And through everything, through Dunfermline and Chicago and San Francisco and the courtroom in Pittsburgh, he kept the name. It is on his stone.

He carried more than one name, and each one was a gift from someone who loved him.

He was born Lawrence Brown, the son of Agnes, who carried him through a frightened winter and could not keep him.

He grew up Laurence Douglas, the son of Robert and Lizzie, who took in another woman's child and loved him as their own.

He crossed the ocean as Lawrence Brown again, because to begin his new life he had to go back to the first one.

And in 1943, in a federal court in Pittsburgh, he became a citizen of the United States as Laurence Brown Douglas: both names at once, the mother who bore him and the family that raised him, side by side.

To his friends, he was Doug.

I set out to give my grandfather a voice. But more than that, I did it for his daughter—my mother, Constance Douglas, who is eighty-five now. She remembered her father too, and when she spoke of him there was always a little more sadness in it than I ever heard from Nan. I wanted to give him back to her: to make his life real and tangible again.

And somewhere along the way, going back through all of it, I learned that there is nothing to be sad about. Laurence Brown Douglas led an amazing life. From the very first he was surrounded by people who loved him and who guided him—none more than his older sister, Janet, who went ahead of him across the ocean and was waiting on the other side. For all the empty spaces on his papers, he was never as alone as they made him look. He was carried, the whole way.

His watch is on my desk as I write this. It is small and heavy and worn smooth at the edges, the way metal gets when it has lived for years in a man's pocket and been taken out a thousand times to check the hour. The family says he carried it across the Atlantic in 1929, and kept it all his life.

I open the case. Inside there is one word, the name of the city where it all began:

Edinburgh.
