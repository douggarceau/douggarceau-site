GEAR GUIDE

The right gear for the gig

There is no best snare drum, only the right one for the situation. An orchestral snare and a rock snare are built, tuned and played for completely different jobs. This guide breaks down the standard setup for each playing situation, and why each choice is made.

Orchestral & concert

In the orchestra the snare has to speak clearly at pianissimo and blend at fortissimo. Everything about the drum is aimed at a crisp, articulate sound with no ring.

Concert snare

Size14" diameter, about 5.5" to 6.5" deep
ShellWood (often maple) or metal; many players keep one of each
SnaresCable, gut or coated-wire snares, often with a strainer that tensions each strand separately
HeadsCalfskin, or a synthetic calf-style head such as the Remo Renaissance
TuningHigh and tight for articulation; snares set so soft rolls stay crisp
WhyRolls and soft passages must be clean and even. Ring and excess wire buzz get lost in a hall or clash with the strings.

The rest of the section is its own instrument family: a concert bass drum (usually 32" to 36"), suspended and crash (clash) cymbals, timpani, and mallet keyboards. Orchestral players are expected to cover all of it.

Rock

Rock needs volume, a fat backbeat and projection over amplified guitars. The drums are bigger, the heads are thicker, and the tuning is lower.

Rock snare

Size14" x 6.5" is the standard; 14" x 5" for a tighter crack
ShellMetal (brass, bronze or steel) for cut; the Ludwig Black Beauty (brass) and Supraphonic (aluminum) are the classic references
HeadsCoated single-ply for an open sound, or a thicker two-ply batter for a fat, controlled backbeat
TuningMedium to low, with enough tension that rimshots bark
WhyThe backbeat is the loudest thing on the kit and has to sit on top of a loud band night after night.

Rock kit

Bass drum22" or 24", often with a port in the front head
Toms12" or 13" rack; 16" (and often 18") floor
Cymbals20"-22" medium or heavy ride, 14"-15" hi-hats, 16"-19" crashes
WhyBigger drums move more air and punch through amplified guitars.

Jazz

Jazz is the opposite: a small, high-tuned kit that sits inside an acoustic band and lets the ride cymbal do the talking.

Jazz kit

Bass drum18" (sometimes 20"), tuned up like a big tom, often with no port
Toms12" rack, 14" floor (the classic bebop kit)
Snare14" x 5" or 5.5" wood, coated single-ply heads, tuned high
Cymbals20"-22" thin or medium-thin dark ride, 14" hi-hats; many players add a sizzle cymbal
WhyAn 18" kick tuned high sounds like part of the band, not a thump underneath it. The tone of the ride is the sound of the drummer.

Not every jazz drummer follows the small-kit rule. Elvin Jones played an 18" bass drum with two rack toms and two floor toms, including an 18" floor tom as big as the kick, and used the extra drums for the rolling triplet phrases that defined his sound. You can switch to his setup on the Level 7 kit.

Studio & session

A session player's job is to deliver whatever sound the record needs, so the kit is a toolbox rather than one instrument.

Session setup

SnaresSeveral: a deep metal snare, a shallow wood snare, and something dry or trashy. The Ludwig Supraphonic 14" x 5" is one of the most recorded snares ever made
HeadsChosen per song: coated for warmth, clear for attack, pre-muffled for control
MufflingGel pads, tape, or a towel on the snare for the dead, close-miked sound of 1970s records
CymbalsSeveral rides and hi-hats to match the key, era and density of the track
WhyThe producer hears the sound in the control room, not the room. Being able to change it in five minutes is the job.

Marching

Marching snare

Size14" x 12", very deep
HeadsKevlar or high-tension synthetic batter heads, cranked extremely tight
WhyOutdoors there is no room to help the sound. High tension gives the crack and articulation that carry across a field.

Sticks & beaters

Choosing sticks

7ALight and thin. Jazz, low-volume and small rooms
5AThe all-rounder. Most students start here
5BHeavier. Rock and louder situations
2BHeavy. Loud rock, and building strength on the pad
BrushesWire or nylon. Ballads, swing and anywhere sticks are too loud
MalletsSoft felt for cymbal swells and tom rolls; orchestral players carry many hardnesses

Sizes vary a little between makers, so try the same size from two brands before settling.

Where to look

For specs and current models, go to the makers directly: Ludwig (snares and kits), Remo (heads), Zildjian and Sabian (cymbals), and Vic Firth (sticks, brushes and mallets).
