THE PERCUSSION ENSEMBLE

The Drum Room

Everything a percussionist plays in a school band, orchestra or percussion ensemble, in one room. Tap any button to hear the instrument. The keyboard instruments play real notes, and "Play all" runs up the scale.

The drum set

Play the kit →

Bass drum, snare, toms, hi-hats, crash and ride, all played by one drummer. Tap it, and play along with nine time feels on Level 7.

Keyboard percussion

Pitched instruments laid out like a piano keyboard, played with mallets. Every school percussion section has these, and they are how percussionists learn to read melody.

Xylophone

Bright, dry and short. The xylophone cuts through a full band, which is why it gets fast, sparkling melody lines.

Bars
Rosewood or synthetic
Range
About 3½ octaves, sounding one octave above written
Mallets
Hard rubber or plastic

Marimba

The warm, deep cousin of the xylophone. Marimba players often hold four mallets at once to play chords.

Bars
Rosewood or synthetic, with tube resonators
Range
4⅓ to 5 octaves, sounding as written
Mallets
Yarn, from soft to hard

Glockenspiel (Orchestra Bells)

The small steel bells that ring over the top of the band. Usually the first mallet instrument a young percussionist plays.

Bars
Steel
Range
About 2½ octaves, sounding two octaves above written
Mallets
Brass, hard plastic or rubber

Vibraphone

Metal bars that ring and sustain, with a motor that makes the sound shimmer. The vibes are a jazz favorite.

Bars
Aluminum, with resonators and a motor
Range
3 octaves, F3 to F6
Pedal
Damper pedal sustains the notes

Chimes (Tubular Bells)

Long hanging tubes that sound like church bells. Used for big moments, like the clock striking in an orchestral piece.

Tubes
Brass, hung in a frame
Range
1½ octaves, C4 to F5
Played with
Rawhide or plastic hammers

Timpani

Large copper kettledrums that play real pitches. A pedal changes the tension of the head, so the player can retune in the middle of a piece.

Timpani (Kettledrums)

The timpani usually play the most important notes of the harmony, and they can roll from a whisper to a roar. Tap each drum below; the notes are one example tuning.

Standard set
Four drums: 32", 29", 26" and 23"
Tuning
Pedal changes the pitch
Mallets
Felt, from soft to hard

Concert drums

The drums of the band and orchestra. Same family as the drum set, but played standing and with a different touch.

Concert Snare Drum

The core instrument of percussion training. Concert snare playing is all about control, clean rolls and dynamics, which is where the rudiments come in.

Size
Usually 14" across
Sound
Wires (snares) under the bottom head buzz
Sticks
Concert snare sticks

Concert Bass Drum

Much bigger than a drum set bass drum. It can play a soft rumble under the orchestra or a huge single hit.

Size
About 32" to 36" across
Stand
Tilting stand, played standing
Beaters
Large soft beaters

Concert Toms

A row of tuned drums for melodic drum parts and big fills in band music.

Set
Often four to eight drums, high to low
Heads
Usually single-headed
Sticks
Sticks or mallets

Cymbals and gongs

Metal instruments that crash, shimmer and ring.

Crash Cymbals (Pair)

The big, bright crash at the climax of a march. Getting a clean crash with no air pocket takes real practice.

Size
Usually 16" to 20"
Played
Two cymbals held by straps and struck together
Tip
Let them ring, then damp against the body

Suspended Cymbal

A single cymbal played with mallets. The swelling roll that builds into a big moment comes from this instrument.

Mounted
A single cymbal on a stand
Played with
Yarn mallets or sticks
Classic sound
A mallet roll that swells

Tam-Tam

The giant hanging gong. Its sound blooms after it is struck and can rumble for a very long time.

Size
Often 32" to 40" across
Pitch
No single definite pitch
Beater
Large soft beater

Accessory percussion

The small instruments that give a percussion section its color. Every one of them has its own technique.

Triangle

A clear, ringing tone that floats over the whole ensemble.

Made of
A bent steel rod
Beater
Metal beater
Tip
Hold it by a thin loop so it rings freely

Tambourine

Bright jingles for dances and marches.

Made of
A frame with metal jingles, often with a head
Played
Struck, shaken or thumb rolled
Tip
Hold it at an angle so the jingles stay quiet until struck

Wood Block

The ticking clock and the galloping horse of the percussion section.

Made of
A hollow block of hardwood or plastic
Beater
Hard rubber mallet or stick
Sound
A sharp, hollow click

Temple Blocks

A set of pitched wood blocks, famous for clip-clop horse sounds.

Set
Usually five blocks, high to low
Beater
Hard rubber mallets
Sound
Hollow, pitched clops

Claves

The heartbeat of Latin music. The clave rhythm is the same one behind the Bo Diddley beat.

Made of
Two hardwood sticks
Played
One struck against the other, cupped in the hand
Sound
A high, piercing click

Castanets

The clicking sound of Spanish dance music.

Made of
Two shells of hardwood
In the ensemble
Usually mounted on a paddle or block
Sound
Quick, dry clicks

Maracas

A pair of shakers that keep a steady pulse in Latin music.

Made of
Gourds or shells filled with seeds or beads
Played
Shaken in a steady rhythm
Sound
A soft, sandy shake

Cowbell

Heard in Latin music, funk and rock alike.

Made of
Steel
Beater
Stick
Sound
A bright, clanking tone

Sleigh Bells

The sound of winter in every holiday concert.

Made of
Small bells on a strap or handle
Played
Shaken or struck
Heard in
Holiday and winter music

Hand drums

Drums played with the hands, from Afro-Cuban music and now heard everywhere.

Bongos

Quick, high-pitched patterns in salsa and Latin jazz.

Set
Two small drums joined together
Names
Macho (small) and hembra (large)
Played
With the fingers, held between the knees

Congas

The deep, singing drums behind salsa, Latin jazz and pop.

Set
Tall drums, usually two or three
Names
Quinto, conga and tumba, high to low
Played
Open tones, slaps and muted strokes
